% Chapter 9: Stochastic differential equations.

The Black--Scholes stock satisfies $\dd S_t=\mu S_t\,\dd t+\sigma S_t\,\dd W_t$: over a short
interval its relative change is a deterministic drift plus a Gaussian shock
(\cref{prop:itf-gbm}). Most continuous-time models in finance have this shape, with
coefficients that depend on the current state: a short rate is pulled back towards a
long-run level, the volatility of a stock rises when its price falls. Such a model is a
\emph{stochastic differential equation}
$\dd X_t=b(t,X_t)\,\dd t+\sigma(t,X_t)\,\dd W_t$. When does it have a solution, and only one?
Which equations can be solved in closed form? How do we simulate the others? On the way we
meet the Markov property and the generator, which lead to the pricing equations of
\cref{ch:10-delta-hedging,ch:12-feynman-kac}.

Throughout, $T>0$ is a fixed horizon, $W$ is a Brownian motion and $(\F_t)$ a filtration for
$W$ (\cref{def:bm-filtration}). We assume that every event of probability zero belongs to
$\F_0$, so that changing a process on a null event does not affect adaptedness. Stochastic
integrals are those of \cref{ch:07-ito-integral}, for integrands in $\mathbb H^2$
(\cref{def:ito-h2}) or in $\mathbb H^2_{\mathrm{loc}}$ (\cref{rem:ito-local}); It\^o
processes and It\^o's formula are those of \cref{ch:08-ito-formula}, where the starting value
of an It\^o process may be any $\F_0$-measurable random variable (\cref{def:itf-process}).

\section{Stochastic differential equations}\label{sec:sde-def}

Let $b,\sigma:[0,T]\times\R\to\R$ be Borel functions, the \emph{drift} and the
\emph{diffusion coefficient}, and let $\xi$ be an $\F_0$-measurable random variable, the
initial condition.

\begin{definition}[Strong solution]\label{def:sde-solution}
A \emph{(strong) solution} of
\begin{equation}\label{eq:sde}
  \dd X_t=b(t,X_t)\,\dd t+\sigma(t,X_t)\,\dd W_t,\qquad X_0=\xi,
\end{equation}
on $[0,T]$ is a process $X=(X_t)_{t\in[0,T]}$ such that
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
  \item $X$ is adapted to $(\F_t)$ and every path $t\mapsto X_t(\omega)$ is continuous;
  \item almost surely, $\int_0^T\big(|b(s,X_s)|+\sigma(s,X_s)^2\big)\,\dd s<\infty$;
  \item almost surely, for every $t\in[0,T]$,
    $X_t=\xi+\int_0^tb(s,X_s)\,\dd s+\int_0^t\sigma(s,X_s)\,\dd W_s$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

Condition (ii) is what (iii) needs. Since $X$ is continuous and adapted, it is
progressively measurable (\textcite{karatzas-shreve}, Proposition~1.1.13), hence so are
$b(s,X_s)$ and $\sigma(s,X_s)$; by (ii), the first is integrable along almost every path and
the second lies in $\mathbb H^2_{\mathrm{loc}}$. A solution is thus an It\^o process
(\cref{def:itf-process}) whose coefficients at time $t$ depend only on $t$ and $X_t$.
\emph{Strong} means that $W$ and $(\F_t)$ are given in advance; in a \emph{weak} solution
they are part of the answer (see the further reading).

The equation is a recipe for the next move. Over a short interval $[t,t+h]$,
\[
  X_{t+h}-X_t\approx b(t,X_t)\,h+\sigma(t,X_t)\,(W_{t+h}-W_t):
\]
given $\F_t$, the increment has mean about $b(t,X_t)h$ and variance about
$\sigma(t,X_t)^2h$. Iterating this approximation on a grid is the Euler--Maruyama scheme of
\cref{sec:sde-euler}.

Geometric Brownian motion is the case $b(t,x)=\mu x$, $\sigma(t,x)=\sigma x$; the
Ornstein--Uhlenbeck and CEV models of \cref{sec:sde-ou,sec:sde-local-vol} are two more.

Even without noise, an equation of this kind can fail to have a solution, or have many.

\begin{example}[What can go wrong]\label{exa:sde-failures}
Take $\sigma=0$, so that \eqref{eq:sde} is an ordinary differential equation.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
  \item \emph{Explosion.} $x'=x^2$, $x(0)=1$, is solved by $x(t)=1/(1-t)$, which explodes
    at $t=1$; the drift grows faster than linearly.
  \item \emph{Branching.} $x'=3|x|^{2/3}$, $x(0)=0$, is solved by $x\equiv0$ and, for every
    $a\ge0$, by $x(t)=((t-a)^+)^3$; the drift is not Lipschitz at $0$.
\end{enumerate}
The next theorem rules these out with a linear-growth bound and a Lipschitz condition.
\end{example}

\section{Existence and uniqueness}\label{sec:sde-existence}

The proofs below bound a mean square distance $\varphi(t)$ by an integral inequality, which
Gronwall's lemma turns into a bound.

\begin{lemma}[Gronwall]\label{lem:sde-gronwall}
Let $\varphi:[0,T]\to[0,\infty)$ be Borel and integrable, and let $a,c\ge0$ be such that
$\varphi(t)\le a+c\int_0^t\varphi(s)\,\dd s$ for every $t\in[0,T]$. Then
$\varphi(t)\le a\,e^{ct}$ for every $t\in[0,T]$. In particular, if $a=0$ then $\varphi\equiv0$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
We show by induction that, for every $n\ge1$ and $t\in[0,T]$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:sde-gronwall-n}
  \varphi(t)\le a\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac{(ct)^k}{k!}
  +c^n\int_0^t\frac{(t-s)^{n-1}}{(n-1)!}\,\varphi(s)\,\dd s .
\end{equation}
For $n=1$ this is the hypothesis. If it holds for $n$, bound $\varphi(s)$ in the last
integral by $a+c\int_0^s\varphi(u)\,\dd u$, and exchange the order of integration (Tonelli):
\[
  c^n\int_0^t\frac{(t-s)^{n-1}}{(n-1)!}\Big(a+c\int_0^s\varphi(u)\,\dd u\Big)\dd s
  =a\frac{(ct)^n}{n!}+c^{n+1}\int_0^t\frac{(t-u)^n}{n!}\,\varphi(u)\,\dd u ,
\]
which gives \eqref{eq:sde-gronwall-n} for $n+1$. The last term of
\eqref{eq:sde-gronwall-n} is at most $c^nT^{n-1}\int_0^T\varphi/(n-1)!$, which tends to $0$,
and the sum tends to $ae^{ct}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Maximal estimates]\label{lem:sde-estimates}
Let $t\in[0,T]$ and let $H$ be progressively measurable with
$\E\int_0^tH_s^2\,\dd s<\infty$. Then
\[
  \E\Big[\sup_{u\le t}\Big|\int_0^uH_s\,\dd s\Big|^2\Big]\le t\,\E\int_0^tH_s^2\,\dd s,
  \qquad
  \E\Big[\sup_{u\le t}\Big|\int_0^uH_s\,\dd W_s\Big|^2\Big]\le 4\,\E\int_0^tH_s^2\,\dd s .
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The first bound is the Cauchy--Schwarz inequality,
$|\int_0^uH_s\,\dd s|^2\le u\int_0^uH_s^2\,\dd s\le t\int_0^tH_s^2\,\dd s$. For the second,
$I_u=\int_0^uH_s\,\dd W_s$ is a continuous martingale on $[0,t]$ with
$\E[I_t^2]=\E\int_0^tH_s^2\,\dd s$ (\cref{thm:ito-integral}). Doob's maximal inequality for
continuous square-integrable martingales gives $\E[\sup_{u\le t}I_u^2]\le4\,\E[I_t^2]$
(\textcite{karatzas-shreve}, Theorem~1.3.8(iv)).
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Existence and uniqueness]\label{thm:sde-existence}
Suppose that there is a constant $K$ such that, for all $t\in[0,T]$ and $x,y\in\R$,
\begin{align}
  |b(t,x)-b(t,y)|+|\sigma(t,x)-\sigma(t,y)|&\le K|x-y|,\label{eq:sde-lipschitz}\\
  |b(t,x)|+|\sigma(t,x)|&\le K(1+|x|).\label{eq:sde-growth}
\end{align}
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
  \item \emph{Pathwise uniqueness.} If $X$ and $Y$ are solutions of \eqref{eq:sde} with the
    same initial condition $\xi$, then $\Prob(X_t=Y_t\text{ for all }t\in[0,T])=1$. Only
    \eqref{eq:sde-lipschitz} is needed for this.
  \item \emph{Existence.} If $\E[\xi^2]<\infty$, then \eqref{eq:sde} has a solution $X$, and
    $\E\big[\sup_{t\le T}X_t^2\big]\le C\big(1+\E[\xi^2]\big)$ for a constant $C$ that
    depends only on $K$ and $T$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
(a) Let $D=X-Y$. Subtracting the two integral equations,
$D_t=\int_0^t\beta_s\,\dd s+\int_0^t\gamma_s\,\dd W_s$ with
$\beta_s=b(s,X_s)-b(s,Y_s)$ and $\gamma_s=\sigma(s,X_s)-\sigma(s,Y_s)$, and
\eqref{eq:sde-lipschitz} gives $|\beta_s|\le K|D_s|$ and $|\gamma_s|\le K|D_s|$. We know
nothing about the moments of $D$, so we do not apply It\^o's formula to $D^2$ but to a
bounded function of $D$: $f(x)=x^2/(1+x^2)$, which takes values in $[0,1)$, vanishes only at
$0$, and has $f'(x)=2x/(1+x^2)^2$ and $f''(x)=(2-6x^2)/(1+x^2)^3$, so that
$|xf'(x)|\le\min(2f(x),\frac12)$ and $x^2|f''(x)|\le6f(x)$.
By It\^o's formula (\cref{thm:itf-process}, with the stochastic integral understood as in
\cref{rem:ito-local}),
\[
  f(D_t)=\int_0^t\Big(f'(D_s)\beta_s+\tfrac12f''(D_s)\gamma_s^2\Big)\dd s
  +\int_0^tf'(D_s)\gamma_s\,\dd W_s .
\]
The integrand of the stochastic integral is bounded by $K|D_sf'(D_s)|\le K/2$, so the
integral is a martingale with mean zero (\cref{thm:ito-integral}). The $\dd s$ integrand is
bounded by $(2K+3K^2)f(D_s)\le2K+3K^2$. Taking expectations and using Fubini,
$\varphi(t)=\E[f(D_t)]$ satisfies $\varphi(t)\le(2K+3K^2)\int_0^t\varphi(s)\,\dd s$. The
function $\varphi$ is bounded by $1$ and continuous (bounded convergence and continuity of
$D$), so Gronwall's lemma with $a=0$ gives $\varphi\equiv0$. Hence, for each $t$, $D_t=0$
almost surely. A countable intersection of almost sure events is almost sure, so almost
surely $D_t=0$ for every rational $t$, and then for every $t$ by continuity.

(b) We construct the solution by \emph{Picard iteration}, as for ordinary differential
equations. For a process $Z$ write $\|Z\|_t^2=\E[\sup_{u\le t}Z_u^2]$. Set $X^0_t=\xi$ and
\[
  X^{n+1}_t=\xi+\int_0^tb(s,X^n_s)\,\dd s+\int_0^t\sigma(s,X^n_s)\,\dd W_s,\qquad t\in[0,T].
\]

\emph{Step 1: the iterates are well defined.} Suppose $X^n$ is continuous, adapted and
$\|X^n\|_T<\infty$ (true for $n=0$). By \eqref{eq:sde-growth},
$b^2+\sigma^2\le(|b|+|\sigma|)^2\le2K^2(1+x^2)$, so
$\E\int_0^T(b^2+\sigma^2)(s,X^n_s)\,\dd s\le2K^2T(1+\|X^n\|_T^2)<\infty$. Hence $X^{n+1}$ is
defined, continuous and adapted, and by \cref{lem:sde-estimates} and
$(u+v+w)^2\le3(u^2+v^2+w^2)$,
$\|X^{n+1}\|_T^2\le3\E[\xi^2]+3(T+4)\E\int_0^T(b^2+\sigma^2)(s,X^n_s)\,\dd s<\infty$.

\emph{Step 2: contraction.} Let $\Delta_n(t)=\|X^{n+1}-X^n\|_t^2$. The difference
$X^{n+1}-X^n$ has drift $b(s,X^n_s)-b(s,X^{n-1}_s)$ and diffusion
$\sigma(s,X^n_s)-\sigma(s,X^{n-1}_s)$, both bounded by $K|X^n_s-X^{n-1}_s|$. By
$(u+v)^2\le2u^2+2v^2$ and \cref{lem:sde-estimates},
\[
  \Delta_n(t)\le2(t+4)K^2\,\E\int_0^t|X^n_s-X^{n-1}_s|^2\,\dd s\le L\int_0^t\Delta_{n-1}(s)\,\dd s,
  \qquad L=2K^2(T+4).
\]
($\Delta_{n-1}$ is nondecreasing, hence Borel.) Since $\Delta_0(t)\le M:=\Delta_0(T)<\infty$,
induction gives $\Delta_n(t)\le M(Lt)^n/n!$.

\emph{Step 3: convergence.} By Chebyshev's inequality,
$\Prob\big(\sup_{t\le T}|X^{n+1}_t-X^n_t|>2^{-n}\big)\le4^nM(LT)^n/n!$, which is summable.
By the Borel--Cantelli lemma, almost surely $\sup_t|X^{n+1}_t-X^n_t|\le2^{-n}$ for all large
$n$, and on this event $X^n$ converges uniformly on $[0,T]$ to a continuous limit $X$. Set
$X=0$ on the exceptional null event, which lies in $\F_0$; then $X$ is adapted. By Fatou's
lemma and the triangle inequality for $\|\cdot\|_T$,
$\|X-X^n\|_T\le\sum_{k\ge n}\sqrt{\Delta_k(T)}\to0$; in particular $\|X\|_T<\infty$.

\emph{Step 4: $X$ solves the equation.} By \eqref{eq:sde-lipschitz} and
\cref{lem:sde-estimates}, as in Step 2,
\[
  \E\Big[\sup_{t\le T}\Big|\int_0^t\big(b(s,X^n_s)-b(s,X_s)\big)\dd s
  +\int_0^t\big(\sigma(s,X^n_s)-\sigma(s,X_s)\big)\dd W_s\Big|^2\Big]\le LT\,\|X^n-X\|_T^2\to0 .
\]
So the right-hand side of the recursion converges, uniformly in $t$ in mean square, to
$\xi+\int_0^tb(s,X_s)\,\dd s+\int_0^t\sigma(s,X_s)\,\dd W_s$, while the left-hand side
$X^{n+1}$ converges to $X$. The two limits agree almost surely for each $t$, and, both being
continuous in $t$, for all $t$ at once. Condition (ii) of \cref{def:sde-solution} holds
because $\E\int_0^T(b^2+\sigma^2)(s,X_s)\,\dd s<\infty$.

\emph{Step 5: the moment bound.} As in Step 1, $\varphi(t)=\|X\|_t^2$ satisfies
$\varphi(t)\le3\E[\xi^2]+6K^2T(T+4)+6K^2(T+4)\int_0^t\varphi(s)\,\dd s$, and Gronwall's lemma
gives $\varphi(T)\le\big(3\E[\xi^2]+6K^2T(T+4)\big)e^{6K^2T(T+4)}$.
\end{proof}

Part (a) is a special case of \textcite{karatzas-shreve}, Theorem~5.2.5, and part (b) is
their Theorem~5.2.9 (which bounds $\sup_t\E[X_t^2]$; the bound on $\E[\sup_tX_t^2]$ is
Step 5). Their theorem also shows that $X_t$ is measurable with respect to
$\sigma(\xi,W_s:s\le t)$ augmented by the null events.

\begin{remark}[On the hypotheses]\label{rem:sde-hypotheses}
\leavevmode
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
  \item The Lipschitz constant must be uniform in $t\in[0,T]$. Given
    \eqref{eq:sde-lipschitz}, the growth bound \eqref{eq:sde-growth} holds, with a possibly larger constant, if and only if
    $|b(t,0)|+|\sigma(t,0)|$ is bounded on $[0,T]$, because
    $|b(t,x)|\le|b(t,0)|+K|x|$. The proof uses \eqref{eq:sde-growth} only in Steps 1, 4 and
    5, and with small changes there it goes through under the weaker condition
    $\int_0^T\big(|b(t,0)|+\sigma(t,0)^2\big)\,\dd t<\infty$; the constant $C$ then also
    depends on this integral.
  \item The Lipschitz condition can be weakened in two useful ways. If $b$ and $\sigma$ are
    only \emph{locally} Lipschitz in $x$ (Lipschitz on every bounded set, with constants
    uniform in $t$), pathwise uniqueness still holds (\textcite{karatzas-shreve},
    Theorem~5.2.5), although a solution on $[0,T]$ need not exist
    (\cref{exa:sde-failures}(a)). In dimension one, it is enough that $b$ be Lipschitz and
    that $|\sigma(t,x)-\sigma(t,y)|\le h(|x-y|)$ for a strictly increasing $h$ with
    $h(0)=0$ and $\int_0^\varepsilon h(u)^{-2}\,\dd u=\infty$ for every $\varepsilon>0$, for
    instance $h(u)=c\sqrt u$, for pathwise uniqueness (not existence): this is the
    Yamada--Watanabe criterion (\textcite{karatzas-shreve}, Proposition~5.2.13), which covers the square-root
    diffusions of \cref{sec:sde-local-vol} and \cref{ch:14-rates}.
\end{enumerate}
\end{remark}

\section{Linear equations}\label{sec:sde-linear}

When the coefficients are affine in $x$, the equation can be solved explicitly, as a linear
ordinary differential equation is solved with an integrating factor.

\begin{proposition}[Linear SDEs]\label{prop:sde-linear}
Let $\mu_0,\mu_1,\sigma_0,\sigma_1:[0,T]\to\R$ be bounded Borel functions and $\xi$ an
$\F_0$-measurable random variable. Let
\[
  Z_t=\exp\Big(\int_0^t\sigma_1(s)\,\dd W_s+\int_0^t\big(\mu_1(s)-\tfrac12\sigma_1(s)^2\big)\dd s\Big).
\]
The equation
\begin{equation}\label{eq:sde-linear}
  \dd X_t=\big(\mu_0(t)+\mu_1(t)X_t\big)\dd t+\big(\sigma_0(t)+\sigma_1(t)X_t\big)\dd W_t,
  \qquad X_0=\xi,
\end{equation}
has the unique solution
\begin{equation}\label{eq:sde-linear-solution}
  X_t=Z_t\Big(\xi+\int_0^t\frac{\mu_0(s)-\sigma_0(s)\sigma_1(s)}{Z_s}\,\dd s
  +\int_0^t\frac{\sigma_0(s)}{Z_s}\,\dd W_s\Big).
\end{equation}
In particular:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
  \item ($\mu_0=\sigma_0=0$, $\mu_1=\mu$, $\sigma_1=\sigma$ constant) geometric Brownian
    motion $S_t=S_0\exp\big(\sigma W_t+(\mu-\frac{\sigma^2}2)t\big)$ is the unique solution of
    $\dd S_t=\mu S_t\,\dd t+\sigma S_t\,\dd W_t$;
  \item ($\sigma_1=0$, additive noise) with $B(t)=\int_0^t\mu_1(s)\,\dd s$,
    \[
      X_t=e^{B(t)}\xi+\int_0^te^{B(t)-B(s)}\mu_0(s)\,\dd s+\int_0^te^{B(t)-B(s)}\sigma_0(s)\,\dd W_s ;
    \]
    if $\xi=x_0$ is a constant, $X_t$ is normal with mean
    $e^{B(t)}x_0+\int_0^te^{B(t)-B(s)}\mu_0(s)\,\dd s$ and variance
    $\int_0^te^{2(B(t)-B(s))}\sigma_0(s)^2\,\dd s$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
\emph{Uniqueness.} The coefficients $(t,x)\mapsto\mu_0(t)+\mu_1(t)x$ and
$\sigma_0(t)+\sigma_1(t)x$ satisfy \eqref{eq:sde-lipschitz} with
$K=\sup|\mu_1|+\sup|\sigma_1|$, so \cref{thm:sde-existence}(a) applies. It requires no
moment of $\xi$.

\emph{The formula is a solution.} By It\^o's formula for the exponential of the It\^o
process $\int_0^t\sigma_1\,\dd W+\int_0^t(\mu_1-\frac12\sigma_1^2)\,\dd s$ (\cref{thm:itf-process}),
$\dd Z_t=Z_t\big(\mu_1(t)\,\dd t+\sigma_1(t)\,\dd W_t\big)$ with $Z_0=1$. Let $U_t$ be the
bracket in \eqref{eq:sde-linear-solution}, so that
$\dd U_t=Z_t^{-1}\big((\mu_0-\sigma_0\sigma_1)\,\dd t+\sigma_0\,\dd W_t\big)$. Since
$\int_0^s\sigma_1\,\dd W$ is normal (\cref{prop:ito-deterministic}) and the coefficients are
bounded, $\E[Z_s^2]$ and $\E[Z_s^{-2}]$ are bounded on $[0,T]$; so $Z$ and $U$ are It\^o
processes with integrands in $\mathbb H^2$. By the product rule
(\cref{cor:itf-product}), with $\dd[Z,U]_t=Z_t\sigma_1(t)\cdot Z_t^{-1}\sigma_0(t)\,\dd t$
(\cref{prop:itf-decomposition}),
\[
  \dd(Z_tU_t)=Z_t\,\dd U_t+U_t\,\dd Z_t+\dd[Z,U]_t
  =\big(\mu_0+\mu_1Z_tU_t\big)\dd t+\big(\sigma_0+\sigma_1Z_tU_t\big)\dd W_t ,
\]
and $Z_0U_0=\xi$. So $X=ZU$ solves \eqref{eq:sde-linear}.

\emph{Special cases.} (a) is the case $Z_t=S_t/S_0$, $U\equiv S_0$. In (b), $Z_t=e^{B(t)}$ is
deterministic and can be moved inside the integrals. The stochastic integral of the
deterministic integrand $e^{B(t)-B(s)}\sigma_0(s)$, $s\le t$, is normal with mean $0$ and
variance $\int_0^te^{2(B(t)-B(s))}\sigma_0(s)^2\,\dd s$ (\cref{prop:ito-deterministic}); the
other two terms are constants.
\end{proof}

The formula comes from the integrating factor $Z^{-1}$:
$\dd(Z_t^{-1}X_t)=Z_t^{-1}\big((\mu_0-\sigma_0\sigma_1)\,\dd t+\sigma_0\,\dd W_t\big)$ no
longer involves $X$; the term $-\sigma_0\sigma_1$ comes from the covariation of $Z^{-1}$ and $X$.

\section{The Ornstein--Uhlenbeck process and the Vasicek model}\label{sec:sde-ou}

\begin{proposition}[Ornstein--Uhlenbeck process]\label{prop:sde-ou}
Let $\kappa>0$, $\theta\in\R$, $\sigma>0$, let $\xi$ be $\F_0$-measurable, and write
$v(t)=\sigma^2(1-e^{-2\kappa t})/(2\kappa)$. The equation
$\dd X_t=\kappa(\theta-X_t)\,\dd t+\sigma\,\dd W_t$, $X_0=\xi$, has the unique solution
\begin{equation}\label{eq:sde-ou}
  X_t=\theta+(\xi-\theta)e^{-\kappa t}+\sigma\int_0^te^{-\kappa(t-s)}\,\dd W_s .
\end{equation}
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
  \item For $0\le t\le u$,
    $X_u=\theta+(X_t-\theta)e^{-\kappa(u-t)}+\sigma\int_t^ue^{-\kappa(u-s)}\,\dd W_s$, and
    the last term is normal with mean $0$ and variance $v(u-t)$, and independent of $\F_t$.
  \item If $\xi=x_0$ is a constant, $X_t$ is normal with mean $\theta+(x_0-\theta)e^{-\kappa t}$
    and variance $v(t)$. As $t\to\infty$, its law converges to
    $\mathcal N\big(\theta,\frac{\sigma^2}{2\kappa}\big)$, whatever $x_0$.
  \item If $\xi\sim\mathcal N\big(\theta,\frac{\sigma^2}{2\kappa}\big)$, then
    $X_t\sim\mathcal N\big(\theta,\frac{\sigma^2}{2\kappa}\big)$ for every $t\ge0$: this is the
    \emph{stationary law}.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

Formula \eqref{eq:sde-ou} gives the solution on every horizon at once, which gives a meaning
to $t\to\infty$ in (ii).

\begin{proof}
This is \cref{prop:sde-linear} with $\mu_0=\kappa\theta$, $\mu_1=-\kappa$,
$\sigma_0=\sigma$, $\sigma_1=0$: then $Z_t=e^{-\kappa t}$ and
\eqref{eq:sde-linear-solution} becomes \eqref{eq:sde-ou}, since
$e^{-\kappa t}\int_0^te^{\kappa s}\kappa\theta\,\dd s=\theta(1-e^{-\kappa t})$.

(i) Write $e^{-\kappa(u-s)}=e^{-\kappa(u-t)}e^{-\kappa(t-s)}$ in \eqref{eq:sde-ou} at time
$u$ and split $\int_0^u=\int_0^t+\int_t^u$; the terms up to time $t$ recombine into
$e^{-\kappa(u-t)}(X_t-\theta)$. Let $J=\int_t^ue^{-\kappa(u-s)}\,\dd W_s$ and, for a partition
$t=s_0<\dots<s_m=u$, $J_m=\sum_je^{-\kappa(u-s_j)}(W_{s_{j+1}}-W_{s_j})$, the integral of a
simple integrand (\cref{def:ito-simple}). Each increment $W_{s_{j+1}}-W_{s_j}$ is normal and
independent of $\F_{s_j}$, which contains $\F_t$ and the earlier increments
(\cref{def:bm-filtration}); so $J_m$ is normal and independent of $\F_t$. As the mesh goes
to $0$, $J_m\to J$ in $L^2$ by the It\^o isometry (the integrands converge uniformly on
$[t,u]$), so for $A\in\F_t$, $\lambda\in\R$, the identity
$\E[\1_Ae^{i\lambda J_m}]=\Prob(A)e^{-\lambda^2\Var(J_m)/2}$ passes to the limit:
$\E[\1_Ae^{i\lambda J}]=\Prob(A)e^{-\lambda^2w/2}$ with
$w=\int_t^ue^{-2\kappa(u-s)}\,\dd s$. Taking $A=\Omega$ gives $J\sim\mathcal N(0,w)$, and
$\sigma^2w=v(u-t)$; for $\Prob(A)>0$ the law of $J$ given $A$ is that of $J$, so $J$ is
independent of $\F_t$.

(ii) Take $t=0$ in (i). As $t\to\infty$, the mean tends to $\theta$ and $v(t)$ to
$\sigma^2/(2\kappa)$; the characteristic functions of $X_t$ converge to that of
$\mathcal N(\theta,\sigma^2/(2\kappa))$, which is convergence in law by Lévy's continuity
theorem.

(iii) By (i) with $t=0$, $X_t=\theta+(\xi-\theta)e^{-\kappa t}+\sigma J$ with $\sigma J$
independent of $\F_0$, hence of $\xi$. A sum of independent normal variables is normal, with mean
$\theta$ and variance $e^{-2\kappa t}\frac{\sigma^2}{2\kappa}+v(t)=\frac{\sigma^2}{2\kappa}$.
\end{proof}

The parameters read directly off the solution. The mean forgets the starting point at
rate $\kappa$: the gap $\E[X_t]-\theta$ halves every $\log2/\kappa$ units of time. The
variance does not grow without bound, as it would for $x_0+\sigma W_t$, but levels off at
$\sigma^2/(2\kappa)$, where the pull towards $\theta$ balances the noise. Both features need
$\kappa>0$: for $\kappa\le0$ the same computation writes $X_t$ as a function of $\xi$ plus an
independent normal variable whose variance tends to infinity, so the characteristic function
of $X_t$ tends to $0$ at every $\lambda\ne0$, and no law stays invariant.

\paragraph{The Vasicek model.} \textcite{vasicek} models the short rate as an
Ornstein--Uhlenbeck process $\dd r_t=\kappa(\theta-r_t)\,\dd t+\sigma\,\dd W_t$: rates are
pulled towards a long-run level, and Gaussian rates make bond prices explicit
(\cref{ch:14-rates}). The price of this tractability is that $\Prob(r_t<0)>0$ for every
$t>0$, by (ii). With the parameters of \cref{fig:sde-ou}
($\theta=4\%$, $\kappa=1.5$, $\sigma=2\%$), the stationary probability of a negative rate is
$N\big(-\theta\sqrt{2\kappa}/\sigma\big)\approx2.7\times10^{-4}$.

\section{The Markov property and the generator}\label{sec:sde-markov}

The coefficients of \eqref{eq:sde} depend on the present state only, not on the past of
the path. Since the future increments of $W$ are independent of the past, the future of $X$
should depend on the past only through the present value $X_t$.

\begin{proposition}[Markov property]\label{prop:sde-markov}
Assume the hypotheses of \cref{thm:sde-existence}, and let $X$ be the solution of
\eqref{eq:sde} with $\E[\xi^2]<\infty$. For $t\in[0,T]$ and $x\in\R$, let $X^{t,x}$ be the
solution on $[t,T]$ of the same equation started at time $t$ from $x$:
$X^{t,x}_u=x+\int_t^ub(s,X^{t,x}_s)\,\dd s+\int_t^u\sigma(s,X^{t,x}_s)\,\dd W_s$. Then for
every bounded Borel $h:\R\to\R$,
\[
  \E\big[h(X_T)\,\big|\,\F_t\big]=g(t,X_t),\qquad\text{where } g(t,x)=\E\big[h(X^{t,x}_T)\big].
\]
In particular $\E[h(X_T)\mid\F_t]=\E[h(X_T)\mid X_t]$: given the present state, the rest of
the information available at time $t$ does not help to predict $h(X_T)$.
\end{proposition}

The process $\tilde W_s=W_{t+s}-W_t$ is a Brownian motion (\cref{prop:bm-invariance}(c)), and
$(\F_{t+s})_{s\ge0}$ is a filtration for it by \cref{def:bm-filtration}(iii). An integral
$\int_t^uH\,\dd W$ equals $\int_0^{u-t}H_{t+s}\,\dd\tilde W_s$: both are limits of the same
sums for simple integrands. So $X^{t,x}$ exists by \cref{thm:sde-existence} on $[t,T]$. The
last statement follows from the first by the tower property. For the general case we cite the
result: it is stated without proof in \textcite{shreve2}, Theorem~6.3.1, and proved for
coefficients that do not depend on $t$, with the Brownian filtration, in
\textcite{oksendal}, Theorem~7.1.2. The difficulty is to show that $X^{t,x}_T$ depends
measurably on $x$, so that $x$ can be replaced by $X_t$. For the equations solved in closed
form, the solution shows the structure directly.

\begin{proof}[Proof for geometric Brownian motion and the Ornstein--Uhlenbeck process]
For geometric Brownian motion,
$S_T=S_t\exp\big(\sigma(W_T-W_t)+(\mu-\frac{\sigma^2}2)(T-t)\big)$, where $S_t$ is
$\F_t$-measurable and $W_T-W_t$ is independent of $\F_t$. The independence lemma
(\cref{lem:pr-independence}) gives $\E[h(S_T)\mid\F_t]=g(t,S_t)$ with
$g(t,x)=\E\big[h\big(x\exp(\sigma(W_T-W_t)+(\mu-\frac{\sigma^2}2)(T-t))\big)\big]$. The
variable inside $h$ is $S^{t,x}_T$, by \cref{prop:sde-linear}(a) applied on $[t,T]$.

For the Ornstein--Uhlenbeck process, \cref{prop:sde-ou}(i) gives
$X_T=\theta+(X_t-\theta)e^{-\kappa(T-t)}+\sigma J$ with $J=\int_t^Te^{-\kappa(T-s)}\,\dd W_s$
independent of $\F_t$, and the same formula with $X_t$ replaced by $x$ is $X^{t,x}_T$. The
independence lemma concludes as before.
\end{proof}

By the Markov property, prices at time $t$ of payoffs $h(S_T)$ are functions $v(t,S_t)$
(\cref{ch:10-delta-hedging} onwards), which solve equations built from the following operator.

\begin{definition}[Generator]\label{def:sde-generator}
The \emph{generator} of \eqref{eq:sde} at time $t$ is the operator that maps
$f\in C^2(\R)$ to the function
\[
  (\mathcal L_tf)(x)=\tfrac12\sigma(t,x)^2f''(x)+b(t,x)f'(x).
\]
When $b$ and $\sigma$ do not depend on $t$ we write $\mathcal L$. The process
$M^f_t=f(X_t)-f(X_0)-\int_0^t(\mathcal L_sf)(X_s)\,\dd s$ is the \emph{local martingale
associated with $f$}; it is a martingale under the integrability condition below.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Martingales of a diffusion]\label{prop:sde-dynkin}
Let $X$ be a solution of \eqref{eq:sde} and $f\in C^2(\R)$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
  \item $M^f_t=\int_0^tf'(X_s)\sigma(s,X_s)\,\dd W_s$, a local martingale, and a martingale if
    $\E\int_0^Tf'(X_s)^2\sigma(s,X_s)^2\,\dd s<\infty$. This holds, for instance, under the
    hypotheses of \cref{thm:sde-existence} with $\E[\xi^2]<\infty$ when $f'$ is bounded.
  \item If $M^f$ is a martingale and $\E|f(X_0)|<\infty$, then
    $\E[f(X_t)]=\E[f(X_0)]+\int_0^t\E[(\mathcal L_sf)(X_s)]\,\dd s$ whenever
    $\int_0^T\E|(\mathcal L_sf)(X_s)|\,\dd s<\infty$ (\emph{Dynkin's formula}).
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
(a) Since $\dd[X,X]_t=\sigma(t,X_t)^2\,\dd t$ (\cref{prop:itf-decomposition}), It\^o's
formula (\cref{thm:itf-process}) gives
$\dd f(X_t)=f'(X_t)\big(b\,\dd t+\sigma\,\dd W_t\big)+\frac12f''(X_t)\sigma^2\,\dd t
=(\mathcal L_tf)(X_t)\,\dd t+f'(X_t)\sigma(t,X_t)\,\dd W_t$. The integrand $f'(X)\sigma(\cdot,X)$
is progressively measurable and, since $f'(X)$ is continuous, square integrable along almost
every path by \cref{def:sde-solution}(ii); so $M^f$ is a local martingale
(\cref{rem:ito-local}), and a martingale when the integrand is in $\mathbb H^2$
(\cref{thm:ito-integral}). If $|f'|\le c$, then by \eqref{eq:sde-growth}
$\E\int_0^Tf'(X_s)^2\sigma(s,X_s)^2\,\dd s\le2c^2K^2\big(T+\int_0^T\E[X_s^2]\,\dd s\big)<\infty$
by \cref{thm:sde-existence}(b).

(b) Take expectations in the definition of $M^f$, using $\E[M^f_t]=0$ and Fubini.
\end{proof}

For example, with $f(x)=x$ the Ornstein--Uhlenbeck generator gives
$\mathcal Lf(x)=\kappa(\theta-x)$, so $m(t)=\E[X_t]$ satisfies
$m(t)=x_0+\int_0^t\kappa(\theta-m(s))\,\dd s$, that is $m'=\kappa(\theta-m)$: the mean
found in \cref{prop:sde-ou} without solving the equation. Dynkin's formula reads: the drift
moves the mean of $f(X_t)$ at rate $bf'$, and the noise adds $\frac12\sigma^2f''$, through the
convexity of $f$.

\begin{remark}[Towards the pricing equations]\label{rem:sde-pde}
Let $u\in C^{1,2}([0,T]\times\R)$ solve $\partial_tu+\mathcal L_tu=0$ with $u(T,x)=h(x)$.
By It\^o's formula, $\dd u(t,X_t)=\partial_xu\,\sigma(t,X_t)\,\dd W_t$; if this local
martingale is a martingale, $u(t,X_t)=\E[h(X_T)\mid\F_t]=g(t,X_t)$ in the notation of
\cref{prop:sde-markov}. This is the Feynman--Kac formula of
\cref{ch:12-feynman-kac}; with discounting and the Black--Scholes generator it becomes the
Black--Scholes equation of \cref{ch:10-delta-hedging}.
\end{remark}

\section{Local volatility and the CEV model}\label{sec:sde-local-vol}

In the Black--Scholes model the volatility of returns is constant, but implied volatilities
depend on the strike (\cref{ch:15-volatility}). The simplest models that reproduce such
prices let the volatility depend on time and on the current price.

\begin{definition}[Local volatility model]\label{def:sde-local-vol}
Let $\mu\in\R$ and let $\sigma:[0,T]\times(0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)$ be Borel. A \emph{local
volatility model} for a stock price is a positive solution $S$, with $S_0>0$, of
\[
  \dd S_t=\mu S_t\,\dd t+\sigma(t,S_t)S_t\,\dd W_t .
\]
In this section $\sigma(t,x)$ is a \emph{relative} volatility, the volatility of returns
$\dd S_t/S_t$; the diffusion coefficient in the sense of \cref{def:sde-solution} is
$x\sigma(t,x)$. The \emph{CEV model} is the case $\sigma(t,x)=\sigma x^{\gamma-1}$, with
constants $\sigma>0$ and $\gamma\ge0$.
\end{definition}

Under a risk-neutral measure $\mu$ becomes the interest rate $r$ (\cref{ch:11-risk-neutral}),
and $\sigma(t,x)$ can be read off call prices by Dupire's formula (\cref{ch:15-volatility}).
If $\sigma$ is bounded and Lipschitz in $\log x$, the model is well posed.

\begin{proposition}[Bounded local volatility]\label{prop:sde-lv}
Suppose that there are constants $M$ and $K$ such that $\sigma(t,x)\le M$ and
$|\sigma(t,x)-\sigma(t,x')|\le K|\log x-\log x'|$ for all $t\in[0,T]$ and $x,x'>0$. Then
for every $S_0>0$ there is exactly one positive solution, and it satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{eq:sde-lv-exp}
  S_t=S_0\exp\Big(\int_0^t\sigma(u,S_u)\,\dd W_u+\int_0^t\big(\mu-\tfrac12\sigma(u,S_u)^2\big)\dd u\Big).
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
\emph{Uniqueness.} Set $a(t,x)=x\sigma(t,x)$ for $x>0$ and $a(t,x)=0$ for $x\le0$. For
$0<x'\le x$, $|a(t,x)-a(t,x')|\le(x-x')\sigma(t,x)+x'K\log(x/x')\le(M+K)(x-x')$, since
$\log y\le y-1$; and $|a(t,x)|\le M|x|$. So the equation with coefficients $\mu x$ and $a$ on
$\R$, which every positive solution solves, satisfies \eqref{eq:sde-lipschitz} and
\eqref{eq:sde-growth}, and has at most one solution by \cref{thm:sde-existence}(a).
\emph{Existence.} The coefficients $\mu-\frac12\sigma(t,e^y)^2$ and $\sigma(t,e^y)$ are
bounded and Lipschitz in $y$ (constants $MK$ and $K$), so \cref{thm:sde-existence} gives a
solution $Y$ of $\dd Y_t=(\mu-\frac12\sigma(t,e^{Y_t})^2)\,\dd t+\sigma(t,e^{Y_t})\,\dd W_t$,
$Y_0=\log S_0$. By It\^o's formula, $S=e^Y$ satisfies
$\dd S_t=S_t\,\dd Y_t+\frac12S_t\,\dd[Y,Y]_t=\mu S_t\,\dd t+\sigma(t,S_t)S_t\,\dd W_t$, and
\eqref{eq:sde-lv-exp} is $S=e^Y$ written out.
\end{proof}

Formula \eqref{eq:sde-lv-exp} is not a closed-form solution, since $S$ appears on both
sides; it shows that $S$ stays positive. In practice such models are simulated or solved
numerically (\cref{sec:sde-euler} and \cref{ch:16-numerics}).

\begin{example}[The CEV model]\label{exa:sde-cev}
In the CEV model, $\dd S_t=\mu S_t\,\dd t+\sigma S_t^\gamma\,\dd W_t$, introduced by Cox
(1975), the volatility of returns $\sigma S^{\gamma-1}$ rises when the price falls if
$\gamma<1$. This is the \emph{leverage effect} observed in equity markets. The volatility function is unbounded for
$\gamma\ne1$, so \cref{prop:sde-lv} does not apply, and \cref{thm:sde-existence} applies only
in two cases. Write the diffusion coefficient as $a(x)=\sigma(x^+)^\gamma$ for $\gamma>0$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
  \item $\gamma=1$ (geometric Brownian motion) and $\gamma=0$ (the linear equation
    $\dd S_t=\mu S_t\,\dd t+\sigma\,\dd W_t$) satisfy both hypotheses. For $\gamma=0$ the
    solution is normal (\cref{prop:sde-linear}(b)), hence negative with positive probability:
    not a stock price in the sense of \cref{def:sde-local-vol}.
  \item For $0<\gamma<1$, $a$ satisfies the growth bound ($x^\gamma\le1+x$) but is not
    Lipschitz at $0$: $a(x)/x=\sigma x^{\gamma-1}\to\infty$ as $x\downarrow0$, so the
    theorem does not apply. For $\frac12\le\gamma<1$, $|a(x)-a(y)|\le\sigma|x-y|^\gamma$,
    and the Yamada--Watanabe criterion (\cref{rem:sde-hypotheses}(b)) gives pathwise
    uniqueness. A solution exists. For $n\ge1$, the truncated coefficients $\mu(x^+\wedge n)$
    and $\sigma(x^+\wedge n)^\gamma$ are bounded and continuous, so the truncated equation has
    a weak solution (\textcite{karatzas-shreve}, Theorem~5.4.22); they satisfy the same
    Lipschitz and H\"older bounds, so pathwise uniqueness holds and gives a strong solution
    $S^n$ (their Proposition~5.2.13 and Corollary~5.3.23). For $m\ge n$ the coefficients agree
    on $(-\infty,n]$, so pathwise uniqueness, applied to the processes stopped at their first
    passage at $n$ (\cref{prop:ito-stopping}), gives $S^m=S^n$ up to that time. The growth
    bounds are uniform in $n$, so the moment bound of Step 5 gives
    $\E[\sup_{t\le T}(S^n_t)^2]\le C$ and $\Prob(\sup_{t\le T}S^n_t\ge n)\le C/n^2\to0$; hence
    $S=\lim_nS^n$ exists on $[0,T]$ and solves the equation. Paths reach $0$ with positive
    probability, by Feller's test (\textcite{karatzas-shreve}, Section~5.5.C;
    \exref{ex:sde-feller} computes the probability for $\gamma=\frac12$, $\mu=0$). They
    stay there: if $\tau$ is the first time $S$ hits $0$, then, since both coefficients vanish
    at $0$ and $\int_0^{t\wedge\tau}H\,\dd W=\int_0^t\1_{\{s\le\tau\}}H_s\,\dd W_s$
    (\cref{prop:ito-stopping}), the stopped process $S_{t\wedge\tau}$ solves the same
    equation, and pathwise uniqueness gives $S_t=S_{t\wedge\tau}$: the firm has defaulted. For
    $0<\gamma<\frac12$ the criterion fails, and the model is completed by declaring $0$
    absorbing, as in the figure.
  \item For $\gamma>1$, $a$ is continuously differentiable, hence locally Lipschitz, and
    pathwise uniqueness holds (\cref{rem:sde-hypotheses}(b)); but $a$ grows faster than
    linearly, so the theorem does not apply. For $\gamma=2$ and $\mu=0$,
    \exref{ex:sde-strict} constructs a positive process that solves the equation with
    respect to a suitable Brownian motion, and shows that it is not a martingale, although
    it has no drift.
\end{enumerate}
\end{example}

\section{The Euler--Maruyama scheme}\label{sec:sde-euler}

Most equations have no closed-form solution, and are solved by simulation. Fix $n\ge1$, the
step $h=T/n$ and the grid $t_k=kh$. The \emph{Euler--Maruyama scheme} iterates the
short-time approximation of \cref{sec:sde-def}:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:sde-euler}
  X^h_0=\xi,\qquad
  X^h_{k+1}=X^h_k+b(t_k,X^h_k)\,h+\sigma(t_k,X^h_k)\,(W_{t_{k+1}}-W_{t_k}),\quad k<n .
\end{equation}
To simulate it, the increments $W_{t_{k+1}}-W_{t_k}$ are drawn as independent
$\mathcal N(0,h)$ variables, which is their exact joint law. Two notions of error measure
how well $X^h_n$ approximates $X_T$. The \emph{strong error} $\E|X_T-X^h_n|$ compares the
two on the same Brownian path; it matters when the path itself is used, for example to
follow a hedge along a scenario. The \emph{weak error} $|\E f(X_T)-\E f(X^h_n)|$, for a
function $f$, compares only laws; it is the bias of a Monte Carlo price.

\begin{theorem}[Convergence of the Euler--Maruyama scheme]\label{thm:sde-euler}
\leavevmode
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
  \item \emph{Strong order $\frac12$.} Assume \eqref{eq:sde-lipschitz}, \eqref{eq:sde-growth},
    $\E[\xi^2]<\infty$, and
    $|b(s,x)-b(t,x)|+|\sigma(s,x)-\sigma(t,x)|\le K(1+|x|)|s-t|^{1/2}$ for $s,t\in[0,T]$,
    $x\in\R$. Then there is a constant $C$ such that $\E|X_T-X^h_n|\le C\,h^{1/2}$ for every
    $n$.
  \item \emph{Weak order $1$.} Assume that $b$ and $\sigma$ do not depend on $t$, are four
    times continuously differentiable with bounded first derivatives and derivatives up to
    order four of polynomial growth, and that $\xi=x_0$ is a constant. Let $f\in C^4(\R)$,
    with $f$ and its first four derivatives of polynomial growth. Then there is a constant
    $C_f$ such that $|\E f(X_T)-\E f(X^h_n)|\le C_f\,h$ for every $n$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

Part (a) is \textcite{kloeden-platen}, Theorem~10.2.2, and part (b) is a special case of
their Theorem~14.1.5. The proof of (a) follows the pattern of \cref{thm:sde-existence}:
compare $X$ with the continuous-time interpolation
$Y_t=\xi+\int_0^tb(\eta_s,Y_{\eta_s})\,\dd s+\int_0^t\sigma(\eta_s,Y_{\eta_s})\,\dd W_s$ of the
scheme, where $\eta_s=t_k$ for $s\in[t_k,t_{k+1})$, and apply Gronwall's lemma to
$\E[\sup_{u\le t}|X_u-Y_u|^2]$. The order comes from the one-step bound
$\E|Y_s-Y_{\eta_s}|^2\le Ch$: over a step the path moves by about $\sqrt h$.

\begin{example}[Euler--Maruyama for geometric Brownian motion]\label{exa:sde-euler-gbm}
For $\dd S_t=\mu S_t\,\dd t+\sigma S_t\,\dd W_t$, the scheme multiplies the price at each step
by $1+\mu h+\sigma(W_{t_{k+1}}-W_{t_k})$, so
$S^h_n=S_0\prod_{k<n}\big(1+\mu h+\sigma(W_{t_{k+1}}-W_{t_k})\big)$. The factors are
independent with mean $1+\mu h$, hence $\E[S^h_n]=S_0(1+\mu h)^n$, while
$\E[S_T]=S_0e^{\mu T}$. With $n=T/h$ and $\log(1+\mu h)=\mu h-\frac12\mu^2h^2+O(h^3)$,
\[
  \E[S_T]-\E[S^h_n]=S_0e^{\mu T}\Big(1-e^{-\mu^2Th/2+O(h^2)}\Big)
  =\tfrac12S_0e^{\mu T}\mu^2T\,h+O(h^2) .
\]
For $\mu\ne0$ the weak error for $f(x)=x$ is of order exactly $h$; for $\mu=0$ it vanishes,
since both means equal $S_0$. Note also that a factor $1+\mu h+\sigma(W_{t_{k+1}}-W_{t_k})$
is negative with positive probability: the scheme can produce negative prices, which the
exact solution never does. Applying the scheme to $\log S$, which is a Brownian motion with
drift, avoids both problems for this model.
\end{example}

  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.62\textwidth]{ch09-euler}
  \caption{Errors of the Euler--Maruyama scheme for geometric Brownian motion with $S_0=1$,
    $\mu=0.8$, $\sigma=0.5$, $T=1$, for $h=2^{-1},\dots,2^{-10}$. Each scheme runs on the

  \label{fig:sde-euler}
\end{figure}

Heuristically, the one-step error has a part of root-mean-square size $h$ and mean close
to $0$, and a part of size $h^2$: path by path, $n=T/h$ roughly independent errors of size
$h$ add up to $\sqrt{Th}$, while in the mean only the $n$ terms of size $h^2$ survive. For pricing, the weak order is
the relevant one: a Monte Carlo price has a bias of order $h$ and a statistical error of
order $M^{-1/2}$ for $M$ paths (\cref{ch:16-numerics}). The strong order matters for path-dependent uses and for
multilevel Monte Carlo (Giles, 2008), which couples simulations with different steps. Higher-order schemes add terms from the It\^o--Taylor expansion of the coefficients;
the first of them, the Milstein scheme, has strong order $1$ (\exref{ex:sde-milstein}).
