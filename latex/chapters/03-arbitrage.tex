% Chapter 3: Arbitrage and static replication.

How much can be said about the price of an option without any model for the price of
the underlying asset? More than one might expect. If no portfolio can produce something
out of nothing, the prices of related contracts must satisfy a whole network of
relations: the forward price is fixed, calls and puts are tied by parity, and the price
of a call, as a function of the strike, is nonincreasing, convex, and squeezed between
two lines. Every relation is proved in the same way: compare two payoffs state by state, then
compare their prices. And every relation comes with a trade that makes money when the
relation fails.

The chapter ends with static replication. A smooth payoff $f(S_T)$ is a combination of a
bond, a forward and a continuum of puts and calls (the Carr--Madan formula), so its price
is determined by option prices across strikes. Read the other way, these prices determine
the distribution that the market implicitly assigns to $S_T$. What no-arbitrage arguments
cannot do is single out one price for a call: that takes a model, and
\cref{ch:04-binomial} builds the first one.

\section{Assets, positions and payoffs}\label{sec:arb-market}

There are two dates: today, $0$, and a fixed horizon $T>0$. What happens at $T$ is
uncertain, and the uncertainty is described by a probability space $(\Omega,\F,\Prob)$,
where $\Prob$ is the real-world measure. In this chapter the probabilities themselves play
almost no role: only which events have probability zero will matter. The following assets
are traded at time $0$, at prices known at time $0$.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{The zero-coupon bond} pays $1$ at time $T$ in every state and costs $B>0$ at
    time $0$. Buying bonds is lending; selling bonds is borrowing.
  \item \emph{The stock} costs $S_0>0$ at time $0$ and is worth $S_T$ at time $T$, a random
    variable with $S_T\ge0$ (limited liability: a share cannot be worth less than nothing).
    It pays no dividend between $0$ and $T$.
  \item \emph{European options.} A \emph{call} with strike $K\ge0$ and maturity $T$ gives its
    holder the right, but not the obligation, to buy one share at time $T$ for the price $K$.
    The holder exercises it exactly when $S_T>K$, so the call pays $(S_T-K)^+$. A \emph{put}
    gives the right to sell one share for $K$ and pays $(K-S_T)^+$. Their prices at time $0$
    are $C(K)$ and $P(K)$; the maturity is the same for all options and is left out of the
    notation.
  \item \emph{Forward contracts.} A forward with delivery price $K$ is the obligation to buy
    one share at time $T$ for $K$. It pays $S_T-K$, which can be negative.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Interest convention.} Interest is compounded once per unit of time, as in the
binomial model of \cref{ch:04-binomial}, where the unit of time is one period: a dollar
deposited at time $0$ at the rate $r>-1$ grows to $(1+r)^T$ at time $T$, so
\begin{equation}\label{eq:arb-bond}
  B=(1+r)^{-T}.
\end{equation}
With continuous compounding, used from \cref{ch:06-black-scholes-formula} on, $B=e^{-rT}$
instead. Every result of this chapter is stated in terms of $B$, so it holds verbatim under
either convention. Usually $r\ge0$, that is $B\le1$, but we never use this unless we say so.

\paragraph{Frictionless markets and static portfolios.} We work in an idealized market:
(i) every asset can be bought or sold in any real quantity; (ii) assets are bought and sold
at the same price (no bid--ask spread) and without transaction costs; (iii) every contract
is honoured. A \emph{static portfolio} holds fixed quantities $\theta_1,\dots,\theta_n$ of
traded assets from time $0$ to time $T$. If asset $i$ costs $\pi_i$ and pays $X_i$,
assumptions (i) and (ii) make the cost and the payoff of the portfolio linear:
\[
  v=\sum_{i=1}^n\theta_i\pi_i,\qquad V=\sum_{i=1}^n\theta_iX_i .
\]
A holding $\theta_i>0$ is a \emph{long} position, and $\theta_i<0$ a \emph{short} one. Being
short an option means having written it: the writer of a call receives $C(K)$ today and
pays $(S_T-K)^+$ at $T$. Being short the stock means having borrowed a share and sold it
for $S_0$; at time $T$ the short seller buys a share back for $S_T$ and returns it to the
lender, so the payoff is $-S_T$.

\section{Arbitrage}\label{sec:arb-arbitrage}

\begin{definition}[Arbitrage]\label{def:arb-arbitrage}
A static portfolio with cost $v$ and payoff $V$ is an \emph{arbitrage} if $V\ge0$ almost
surely and either
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
  \item $v<0$, or
  \item $v=0$ and $\Prob(V>0)>0$.
\end{enumerate}
The market is \emph{free of arbitrage} if no portfolio of traded assets is an arbitrage.
\end{definition}

An arbitrage of type (a) brings money today against a position that is never a liability;
one of type (b) is a free lottery ticket. Since the bond is traded, an arbitrage of type (a)
yields one of type (b): adding $-v/B$ bonds gives a portfolio that costs nothing and pays
$V-v/B\ge-v/B>0$ almost surely. (The converse fails in general: a lottery ticket need not
bring money today.) So the market is free of arbitrage exactly when every portfolio that
costs nothing and never loses has a payoff equal to zero almost surely: there is no free
lunch.

\begin{remark}[What the probability does]\label{rem:arb-model-free}
$\Prob$ enters \cref{def:arb-arbitrage} only through its null sets. Replacing $\Prob$ by an
equivalent measure (\cref{def:pr-equivalent}) changes neither the arbitrages nor anything
deduced from their absence. In particular, the probabilities that investors assign to
future states, and their attitudes towards risk, play no role. The relations of
\cref{sec:arb-relations} follow from inequalities between payoffs that hold in every state,
so they hold whatever $\Prob$ is: they are \emph{model-free}.
\end{remark}

\begin{proposition}[No dominance and the law of one price]\label{prop:arb-dominance}
Assume that the market is free of arbitrage, and consider two portfolios with costs
$v,\tilde v$ and payoffs $V,\tilde V$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
  \item (No dominance) If $V\ge\tilde V$ almost surely, then $v\ge\tilde v$. If moreover
    $\Prob(V>\tilde V)>0$, then $v>\tilde v$.
  \item (Law of one price) If $V=\tilde V$ almost surely, then $v=\tilde v$.
\end{enumerate}
In particular, a portfolio whose payoff is nonnegative has a nonnegative cost; and if a
portfolio \emph{replicates} a traded asset, that is, has the same payoff almost surely, its
cost is the price of the asset.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
(i) Hold the first portfolio and short the second. This costs $v-\tilde v$ and pays
$V-\tilde V\ge0$ almost surely. If $v<\tilde v$, it is an arbitrage of type (a); if
$v=\tilde v$ and $\Prob(V>\tilde V)>0$, it is an arbitrage of type (b). Both are excluded.
(ii) Apply (i) twice, exchanging the roles of the two portfolios. The last two claims are
(i) with the empty portfolio as the second portfolio, and (ii) applied to the replicating
portfolio and to the portfolio that holds one unit of the asset.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[From a payoff inequality to a price relation, and back]\label{rem:arb-recipe}
Every result of \cref{sec:arb-relations} follows the same recipe. To prove a price
relation, find two portfolios whose payoffs are ordered in every state, and apply
\cref{prop:arb-dominance}. To exploit a violation of the relation, run the proof backwards:
if $V\ge\tilde V$ in every state but the quotes have $v<\tilde v$, buy the cheap portfolio
and sell the expensive one. This collects $\tilde v-v>0$ today, and the position that
remains, $V-\tilde V$, is never a liability. The art is in finding the two portfolios.
\end{remark}

A second way to rule out arbitrage is to exhibit prices that are expectations.

\begin{definition}[Pricing measure]\label{def:arb-pricing-measure}
A probability measure $\Q$ on $(\Omega,\F)$, equivalent to $\Prob$, is a \emph{pricing
measure} if every traded payoff $X$ is $\Q$-integrable and has price $B\,\EQ[X]$ at time $0$.
\end{definition}

For the stock, $B\,\EQ[S_T]=S_0$: under $\Q$ the stock earns on average the same return as
the bond. This is why a pricing measure is also called a \emph{risk-neutral measure}. When
$\Omega$ is finite and every state has positive probability, $q(\omega)=B\,\Q(\{\omega\})$
is the price that $\Q$ assigns to a claim paying $1$ in the state $\omega$ and nothing
otherwise (it is a market price if that claim is traded or replicable); these numbers are
called \emph{state prices}.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:arb-pricing-measure}
If a pricing measure exists, the market is free of arbitrage. It remains so after adding a
new asset with a $\Q$-integrable payoff $X$ and price $B\,\EQ[X]$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
By linearity, a portfolio with payoff $V$ costs $v=B\,\EQ[V]$. If $V\ge0$ $\Prob$-almost
surely, then also $\Q$-almost surely, because the two measures have the same null sets;
hence $v\ge0$, which excludes type (a). If moreover $v=0$, then $\EQ[V]=0$ for a
nonnegative $V$, so $V=0$ $\Q$-almost surely, hence $\Prob$-almost surely, which excludes
type (b). For the second claim, note that $\Q$ is still a pricing measure for the enlarged
market.
\end{proof}

The converse is the \emph{fundamental theorem of asset pricing}: in a one-period market with
finitely many traded assets, absence of arbitrage implies that a pricing measure exists. It
is proved for the binomial model in \cref{ch:04-binomial} (\cref{thm:bin-ftap}) and in
general in \textcite{follmer-schied}, Section~1.2. \textcite{harrison-pliska} proved the
multi-period version on a finite probability space and developed its continuous-time
counterpart, and \textcite{dalang-morton-willinger} extended the discrete-time theorem to
general probability spaces. We do not need it here. The relations of \cref{sec:arb-relations} use the
absence of arbitrage directly, and pricing measures appear in \cref{sec:arb-replication}
only as an assumption.

\section{Price relations from static arbitrage}\label{sec:arb-relations}

In this section the market of \cref{sec:arb-market} is free of arbitrage. The relations were
derived systematically by \textcite{merton1973} from dominance arguments alone, without any
model for the stock; put--call parity goes back at least to \textcite{stoll1969}.

\begin{proposition}[Forward price]\label{prop:arb-forward}
A forward with delivery price $K$ is worth $S_0-BK$ at time $0$. In particular, the delivery
price that makes it free to enter, the \emph{forward price}, is
\[
  F=\frac{S_0}{B}=S_0(1+r)^T .
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
One share together with $-K$ bonds (a loan of $BK$) pays $S_T-K$ at time $T$, the payoff of
the forward. By the law of one price the forward is worth $S_0-BK$, which vanishes exactly
when $K=S_0/B$.
\end{proof}

The trade behind the proof is the \emph{cash-and-carry}. Suppose that a dealer agrees today,
at no cost, to buy the share at time $T$ for $K>F$. Borrow $S_0$ (sell $S_0/B=F$ bonds), buy
the share, and sell it forward to the dealer. Nothing is paid today; at time $T$ the share
is delivered for $K$ and the loan is repaid with $F$, a sure profit of $K-F$. If $K<F$, do
the opposite: short the share, lend the proceeds, and buy forward.

\begin{remark}[Forwards and futures]
A \emph{futures} contract has the payoff profile of a forward, but gains and losses are paid
every day as the futures price moves (marking to market). Futures and forward prices
coincide when interest rates are deterministic and can differ when they are random; see
\textcite{hull}, Chapter~5. Only forwards appear in these notes.
\end{remark}

\begin{proposition}[Put--call parity]\label{prop:arb-parity}
For every strike $K\ge0$,
\[
  C(K)-P(K)=S_0-BK .
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
For every real $x$, $(x-K)^+-(K-x)^+=x-K$. So a long call and a short put with strike $K$
pay $S_T-K$, the payoff of a forward, and by the law of one price they cost what the forward
costs, $S_0-BK$ by \cref{prop:arb-forward}.
\end{proof}

At the forward price $K=F$ the right-hand side vanishes: a call and a put struck at the
forward have the same price. If the quotes have $C(K)-P(K)>S_0-BK$, the call is expensive
relative to the rest: sell the call, buy the put, buy one share and borrow $BK$. This
collects $C(K)-P(K)-S_0+BK>0$ today, and the position pays
$-(S_T-K)^++(K-S_T)^++S_T-K=0$ in every state. The reverse inequality is traded in reverse.

\begin{proposition}[Bounds]\label{prop:arb-bounds}
For every strike $K\ge0$,
\[
  (S_0-BK)^+\le C(K)\le S_0,\qquad (BK-S_0)^+\le P(K)\le BK .
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
For every $x\ge0$,
\[
  0\le(x-K)^+,\quad x-K\le(x-K)^+\le x,\qquad
  0\le(K-x)^+,\quad K-x\le(K-x)^+\le K,
\]
where both upper bounds use $x\ge0$ (and the first one also $K\ge0$). At $x=S_T$, each inequality compares an option with
nothing, with a long or short forward, with one share, or with $K$ bonds, and
\cref{prop:arb-dominance} turns it into the corresponding inequality between prices. For
example, $(S_T-K)^+\ge S_T-K$ and \cref{prop:arb-forward} give $C(K)\ge S_0-BK$.
\end{proof}

Each bound has its trade. If $C(K)<S_0-BK$, buy the call, short one share and lend $BK$:
this collects $S_0-BK-C(K)>0$, and the position pays $(S_T-K)^+-S_T+K=(K-S_T)^+\ge0$. If
$C(K)>S_0$, sell the call and buy one share: this collects $C(K)-S_0$, and the position pays
$S_T-(S_T-K)^+=\min(S_T,K)\ge0$.

\begin{remark}[Strict bounds and early exercise]\label{rem:arb-strict}
The second half of \cref{prop:arb-dominance}(i) makes the bounds strict when the payoffs
differ with positive probability: $C(K)>0$ if $\Prob(S_T>K)>0$, and $C(K)>S_0-BK$ if
$\Prob(S_T<K)>0$. When $B\le1$, the lower bound gives $C(K)\ge S_0-BK\ge S_0-K$: a European
call on a stock without dividends is worth at least what exercising it immediately would
bring. Applied at every date before maturity, provided the bond price for the remaining
maturity is at most $1$ at every date (interest rates stay nonnegative), this observation of
\textcite{merton1973} shows that nothing is gained by exercising an American call on such a
stock before maturity; American options are the subject of \cref{ch:04-binomial}. Nothing similar holds
for puts, whose lower bound $BK-S_0$ lies below the exercise value $K-S_0$ when $B<1$, nor
for calls on a stock that pays dividends (\exref{ex:arb-dividend}).
\end{remark}

The last group of relations concerns the shape of the price as a function of the strike.

\begin{proposition}[Prices as functions of the strike]\label{prop:arb-strike}
Let $0\le K_1<K_2$ and $\lambda\in[0,1]$. Then
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
  \item (monotonicity) $C(K_1)\ge C(K_2)$ and $P(K_1)\le P(K_2)$;
  \item (slope bounds) $C(K_1)-C(K_2)\le B(K_2-K_1)$ and $P(K_2)-P(K_1)\le B(K_2-K_1)$;
  \item (convexity) $C\big(\lambda K_1+(1-\lambda)K_2\big)\le\lambda C(K_1)+(1-\lambda)C(K_2)$,
    and the same holds for $P$.
\end{enumerate}
Moreover $C(0)=S_0$ and $P(0)=0$. In words: on $[0,\infty)$, the map $K\mapsto C(K)$ is
convex and nonincreasing, with difference quotients in $[-B,0]$, and $K\mapsto P(K)$ is
convex and nondecreasing, with difference quotients in $[0,B]$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Fix $x\ge0$ and look at the call payoff $K\mapsto(x-K)^+$ as a function of the strike. It is
nonincreasing; it is convex, as the maximum of the two affine functions $0$ and $x-K$; and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:arb-spread}
  (x-K_1)^+-(x-K_2)^+=\min\big((x-K_1)^+,\,K_2-K_1\big)\in[0,K_2-K_1] .
\end{equation}
At $x=S_T$ these properties say, in every state: (i) $(S_T-K_1)^+\ge(S_T-K_2)^+$; (ii) the
\emph{call spread}, long a call at $K_1$ and short a call at $K_2$, pays at most $K_2-K_1$,
the payoff of $K_2-K_1$ bonds; (iii) with $K_\lambda=\lambda K_1+(1-\lambda)K_2$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:arb-butterfly}
  \lambda(S_T-K_1)^++(1-\lambda)(S_T-K_2)^+-(S_T-K_\lambda)^+\ge0 .
\end{equation}
\Cref{prop:arb-dominance} turns each of them into the claimed relation for calls. A call
with strike $0$ pays $S_T$, so $C(0)=S_0$ by the law of one price; a put with strike $0$
pays nothing, so $P(0)=0$. The put relations follow in the same way from
$K\mapsto(K-x)^+$, or from parity: $P(K)=C(K)-S_0+BK$ differs from $C(K)$ by an affine
function of $K$ with slope $B$. This preserves convexity, turns (ii) for calls into (i) for
puts, and turns (i) for calls into (ii) for puts.
\end{proof}

Run backwards, as in \cref{rem:arb-recipe}, the proof gives the trades. If (i) fails, buy
the call at $K_1$ and sell the call at $K_2$: a call spread that costs less than nothing. If
(ii) fails, sell that call spread and lend $B(K_2-K_1)$. If (iii) fails, buy the portfolio
in \eqref{eq:arb-butterfly}.

The portfolio in \eqref{eq:arb-butterfly} is a \emph{butterfly}: long $\lambda$ calls at
$K_1$ and $1-\lambda$ calls at $K_2$, short one call at the strike $K_\lambda$ in between.
Its payoff is a tent, zero outside $(K_1,K_2)$ and largest at $K_\lambda$
(\cref{fig:arb-payoffs}). Convexity of $C$ says that a butterfly never has a negative price.

\begin{example}[A butterfly arbitrage]\label{eg:arb-butterfly}
Let $S_0=100$ and $B=0.97$, and suppose that calls with strikes $95$, $100$ and $105$ are
quoted at $8.00$, $5.50$ and $2.60$. The bounds, monotonicity and the slope bounds all hold,
but $\frac12(8.00+2.60)=5.30<5.50$: the middle call is too expensive. Sell two calls with
strike $100$ and buy one call with strike $95$ and one with strike $105$. This collects
$11.00-10.60=0.40$ today. At time $T$ the position pays
$(S_T-95)^+-2(S_T-100)^++(S_T-105)^+$, which is zero outside $(95,105)$, rises to $5$ at
$S_T=100$, and is never negative.
\end{example}

\paragraph{Checking a quoted curve.} In practice calls are quoted at finitely many strikes
$0<K_1<\dots<K_n$. Put $K_0=0$ and $c_0=S_0$ (the stock is the call with strike $0$), let
$c_i$ be the quote at $K_i$, and let
\[
  s_i=\frac{c_i-c_{i-1}}{K_i-K_{i-1}},\qquad i=1,\dots,n,
\]
be the slopes of the piecewise-linear interpolation of the quotes.
\Cref{prop:arb-bounds,prop:arb-strike} require
\begin{equation}\label{eq:arb-checks}
  -B\le s_1\le s_2\le\dots\le s_n\le0\qquad\text{and}\qquad c_n\ge0 .
\end{equation}
Conversely, these finitely many conditions imply every inequality of
\cref{prop:arb-bounds,prop:arb-strike} between quoted strikes: a piecewise-linear function
with nondecreasing slopes is convex, and, for instance,
$c_i=S_0+\sum_{j\le i}s_j(K_j-K_{j-1})\ge S_0-BK_i$. Whether quotes satisfying
\eqref{eq:arb-checks} allow an arbitrage depends on $\Prob$: with $S_0=100$, $B=1$ and
$C(100)=5$ they hold, yet if $\Prob(S_T\le100)=1$, selling the call is an arbitrage. The right
question is whether there is always a model in which such quotes are arbitrage-free. Yes,
except in one boundary case.

\begin{proposition}[The relations are complete]\label{prop:arb-sufficient}
Let $c_0=S_0,c_1,\dots,c_n$ satisfy \eqref{eq:arb-checks}, with $c_n=0$ if $s_n=0$. Then
there are finitely many points $x_j\ge0$ and weights $q_j>0$ such that
\[
  \sum_jq_j=B,\qquad \sum_jq_jx_j=S_0,\qquad \sum_jq_j(x_j-K_i)^+=c_i\quad(i=1,\dots,n).
\]
Consequently, in the model where $S_T$ takes the values $x_j$ with probabilities $q_j/B$,
$\Q=\Prob$ is a pricing measure for the bond, the stock and the calls with prices $c_i$, and
this market is free of arbitrage.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Put weight $B+s_1$ at $0$, weight $s_{i+1}-s_i$ at $K_i$ for $1\le i\le n-1$, and, if
$s_n<0$, weight $-s_n$ at $x_*=K_n+c_n/(-s_n)$. By \eqref{eq:arb-checks} all weights are
nonnegative; discard those that vanish. The total weight telescopes to $B$. Let
$G(K)=\sum_jq_j(x_j-K)^+$. On each interval $[K_{i-1},K_i]$, $1\le i\le n$, no point $x_j$
lies strictly inside, so $G$ is affine there, with slope
\[
  -\sum_{x_j\ge K_i}q_j=-\Big(\sum_{l=i}^{n-1}(s_{l+1}-s_l)-s_n\Big)=s_i .
\]
Moreover $G(K_n)=-s_n(x_*-K_n)=c_n$ if $s_n<0$, and $G(K_n)=0=c_n$ if $s_n=0$. Two functions
that are affine with the same slope on each $[K_{i-1},K_i]$ and agree at $K_n$ agree at every
$K_i$. Hence $G(K_i)=c_i$ for $i\ge1$, and $G(0)=\sum_jq_jx_j=c_0=S_0$. The last claim is
\cref{lem:arb-pricing-measure}.
\end{proof}

If $K_{i-1},K_i,K_{i+1}$ are equally spaced (for $i=1$ this means $K_1=K_2-K_1$, since
$K_0=0$), the weight $s_{i+1}-s_i$ at $K_i$ is $(c_{i+1}-2c_i+c_{i-1})/(K_{i+1}-K_i)$: the
price of a butterfly, divided by the spacing. In the boundary case $s_n=0<c_n$ the last call
spread costs nothing and pays off when $S_T>K_{n-1}$; it is an arbitrage in every model in
which $\Prob(S_T>K_{n-1})>0$, and in the other models selling the last call is one, but there
need not be a single portfolio that is an arbitrage in every model (for an instance, see
\exref{ex:arb-weak}). Apart from this case, every finite set of quotes satisfying
\eqref{eq:arb-checks}, that is, compatible with \cref{fig:arb-call-bounds}, is consistent
with some model: no-arbitrage alone gives relations between prices, not prices. (For a
continuum of strikes one must add $C(K)\to0$ as $K\to\infty$, which holds under any pricing
measure by dominated convergence.)

\section{Static replication}\label{sec:arb-replication}

The relations of \cref{sec:arb-relations} came from payoffs built out of a few calls.
Conversely, many payoffs can be built out of calls. We start with piecewise-linear ones.

\begin{lemma}[Piecewise-linear payoffs]\label{lem:arb-piecewise}
Let $g:[0,\infty)\to\R$ be continuous and piecewise linear, with kinks only at
$0<K_1<\dots<K_m$, and let $w_i=g'(K_i+)-g'(K_i-)$ be the change of slope at $K_i$. Then
\[
  g(x)=g(0)+g'(0+)\,x+\sum_{i=1}^mw_i(x-K_i)^+\qquad\text{for all }x\ge0 .
\]
Hence $g(S_T)$ is replicated by $g(0)$ bonds, $g'(0+)$ shares and $w_i$ calls with strike
$K_i$, and its price is $Bg(0)+g'(0+)S_0+\sum_iw_iC(K_i)$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Both sides are continuous and piecewise linear with kinks only at the $K_i$. They agree at
$0$ and have the same slope on $[0,K_1]$, and their slopes jump by the same amount $w_i$ at
each $K_i$, because $(x-K_i)^+$ is linear away from $K_i$ and its slope jumps by $1$ there. So
they have the same slope on every interval and agree everywhere. The price follows from the
law of one price.
\end{proof}

The forward, the call spread and the butterfly are examples; \exref{ex:arb-payoffs} has
more. A smooth payoff is a limit of piecewise-linear ones, in which the changes of slope
$w_i$ become $f''(K)\,\dd K$. It is convenient to expand around a point $\kappa>0$ rather than
around $0$, with puts below $\kappa$ and calls above.

\begin{theorem}[Carr--Madan formula]\label{thm:arb-carr-madan}
Let $f:(0,\infty)\to\R$ be twice continuously differentiable and $\kappa>0$. For every $x>0$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:arb-carr-madan}
  f(x)=f(\kappa)+f'(\kappa)(x-\kappa)+\int_0^\kappa f''(K)\,(K-x)^+\,\dd K
  +\int_\kappa^\infty f''(K)\,(x-K)^+\,\dd K .
\end{equation}
For each $x$, at most one of the two integrals is nonzero, and it is the integral of a
continuous function over the bounded interval between $x$ and $\kappa$. If $f$ is twice
continuously differentiable on $[0,\infty)$, \eqref{eq:arb-carr-madan} also holds at $x=0$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
We use Taylor's formula with integral remainder: for $x,\kappa>0$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:arb-taylor}
  f(x)=f(\kappa)+f'(\kappa)(x-\kappa)+\int_\kappa^xf''(K)\,(x-K)\,\dd K ,
\end{equation}
which holds because an integration by parts gives
\[
  \int_\kappa^xf''(K)(x-K)\,\dd K=\Big[f'(K)(x-K)\Big]_{K=\kappa}^{K=x}+\int_\kappa^xf'(K)\,\dd K
  =-f'(\kappa)(x-\kappa)+f(x)-f(\kappa).
\]
If $x\ge\kappa$, then $x-K=(x-K)^+$ for $K\in[\kappa,x]$ and $(x-K)^+=0$ for $K>x$, so the
remainder in \eqref{eq:arb-taylor} is the call integral of \eqref{eq:arb-carr-madan}; the put
integral vanishes because $(K-x)^+=0$ for $K\le\kappa\le x$. If $x<\kappa$, the remainder is
$\int_x^\kappa f''(K)(K-x)\,\dd K$, which is the put integral because $(K-x)^+=0$ for $K\le x$;
the call integral vanishes because $(x-K)^+=0$ for $K\ge\kappa>x$. If $f$ is twice
continuously differentiable on $[0,\infty)$, the same computation applies at $x=0$.
\end{proof}

Read as a portfolio, \eqref{eq:arb-carr-madan} replicates $f(S_T)$ statically with
$f(\kappa)$ bonds, $f'(\kappa)$ forwards with delivery price $\kappa$, and, for every strike
$K$, $f''(K)\,\dd K$ puts if $K<\kappa$ and calls if $K>\kappa$. All the options are out of
the money with respect to $\kappa$. The usual choice is the forward price, $\kappa=F$, for
which the forwards cost nothing (\cref{prop:arb-forward}). The proof uses only Taylor's
formula, which holds more generally whenever $f'$ is locally absolutely continuous on
$(0,\infty)$, with $f''$ its derivative defined almost everywhere. The piecewise-linear payoffs of
\cref{lem:arb-piecewise} are the discrete counterpart: a kink of size $w_i$ at $K_i$ plays the
role of $f''(K)\,\dd K$.

\paragraph{Finitely many strikes.} Markets quote finitely many strikes. Let
$k_0<k_1<\dots<k_m$ be a grid of positive strikes with spacing at most $h$, and let $f_h$ be
the continuous piecewise-linear function equal to $f$ at the grid points and extended
linearly beyond $k_0$ and $k_m$. By \cref{lem:arb-piecewise}, $f_h(S_T)$ is replicated exactly
by bonds, shares and calls at the grid strikes; by parity, the calls with strikes below
$\kappa$ can be exchanged for puts, as in \eqref{eq:arb-carr-madan}. The weight at $k_i$ is the
change of slope of $f_h$, which for equal spacing is
$\big(f(k_{i+1})-2f(k_i)+f(k_{i-1})\big)/h\approx f''(k_i)\,h$: a Riemann sum for the strip.
The replication error vanishes at the grid points and, between two of them, is bounded by
\begin{equation}\label{eq:arb-interp-error}
  |f(x)-f_h(x)|\le\frac{h^2}{8}\max_{[k_{i-1},k_i]}|f''|,\qquad x\in[k_{i-1},k_i].
\end{equation}
To see this, take $x\in(a,b)=(k_{i-1},k_i)$ and $E=f(x)-f_h(x)$. The function
$\varphi(t)=f(t)-f_h(t)-E\frac{(t-a)(t-b)}{(x-a)(x-b)}$ vanishes at $a$, $x$ and $b$, so by
Rolle's theorem, applied twice, $\varphi''(\xi)=0$ for some $\xi\in(a,b)$. That is,
$E=\frac12f''(\xi)(x-a)(x-b)$, and $|(x-a)(x-b)|\le(b-a)^2/4$. Outside $[k_0,k_m]$ the error is
that of a linear extrapolation and need not be small: the strikes must cover the region
where $S_T$ is likely to end up (\cref{fig:arb-carr-madan}).

To turn \eqref{eq:arb-carr-madan} into a price, we need to price a portfolio holding a
continuum of options. A pricing measure makes this precise.

\begin{corollary}[Pricing with a strip of options]\label{cor:arb-strip}
Let $\Q$ be a pricing measure for a market in which calls and puts with every strike $K>0$
are traded, and assume $\Q(S_T>0)=1$. Let $f$ and $\kappa$ be as in \cref{thm:arb-carr-madan},
and assume that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:arb-strip-integrable}
  \int_0^\kappa|f''(K)|\,P(K)\,\dd K+\int_\kappa^\infty|f''(K)|\,C(K)\,\dd K<\infty .
\end{equation}
Then $f(S_T)$ is $\Q$-integrable, and its price $B\,\EQ[f(S_T)]$ (\cref{lem:arb-pricing-measure})
is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:arb-strip}
  Bf(\kappa)+f'(\kappa)(S_0-B\kappa)+\int_0^\kappa f''(K)\,P(K)\,\dd K
  +\int_\kappa^\infty f''(K)\,C(K)\,\dd K .
\end{equation}
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
We use two results on exchanging an expectation with an integral in $K$, for a function
$g(K,\omega)$ that is jointly measurable (here it is continuous in $K$): \emph{Tonelli's
theorem}, by which $\EQ\big[\int g\,\dd K\big]=\int\EQ[g]\,\dd K$ whenever $g\ge0$, and
\emph{Fubini's theorem}, by which the same identity holds whenever
$\int\EQ\big[|g|\big]\,\dd K<\infty$; see \textcite{durrett}, Section~1.7. Write $X=S_T$, so
that $P(K)=B\,\EQ[(K-X)^+]$ and $C(K)=B\,\EQ[(X-K)^+]$. By Tonelli's theorem,
$\EQ\big[\int_0^\kappa|f''(K)|(K-X)^+\,\dd K\big]=B^{-1}\int_0^\kappa|f''(K)|P(K)\,\dd K<\infty$,
and likewise for the call integral. So both integrals in \eqref{eq:arb-carr-madan}, evaluated
at $x=X$, are $\Q$-integrable, and so is $f(X)$ by \eqref{eq:arb-carr-madan}, since $X$ is.
Take $\EQ$ in \eqref{eq:arb-carr-madan}, exchange $\EQ$ with the integrals in $K$ by
Fubini's theorem, multiply by $B$ and use $B\,\EQ[X]=S_0$.
\end{proof}

The price \eqref{eq:arb-strip} involves only market prices. A pricing measure is needed to
give a meaning to a portfolio of infinitely many options, but which one it is does not
matter: all pricing measures that reproduce the same option prices give $f(S_T)$ the same
price. Read in the other direction, option prices across strikes pin down the law of $S_T$
under $\Q$.

\begin{proposition}[Breeden--Litzenberger]\label{prop:arb-breeden-litzenberger}
Let $\Q$ be a pricing measure for a market in which calls with every strike $K>0$ are traded.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
  \item For every $K>0$, the call price has one-sided derivatives
    \[
      \partial^+C(K)=-B\,\Q(S_T>K),\qquad \partial^-C(K)=-B\,\Q(S_T\ge K).
    \]
  \item If the law of $S_T$ under $\Q$ has a density $q_T$ that is continuous on an open
    interval $I\subseteq(0,\infty)$, then $C$ is twice continuously differentiable on $I$ and
    \[
      C''(K)=B\,q_T(K),\qquad K\in I .
    \]
  \item Conversely, if $C$ is differentiable on an open interval $I\subseteq(0,\infty)$ and
    $C'$ is locally absolutely continuous on $I$, then on $I$ the law of $S_T$ under $\Q$ has
    the density $C''/B$: $\Q(a<S_T\le b)=B^{-1}\int_a^bC''(K)\,\dd K$ for $a<b$ in $I$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
(i) For $h>0$, \eqref{eq:arb-spread} gives
\[
  \frac{C(K+h)-C(K)}{h}=-B\,\EQ\Big[\frac{\min\big((S_T-K)^+,h\big)}{h}\Big].
\]
The variable inside the expectation takes values in $[0,1]$ and converges to
$\1_{\{S_T>K\}}$ as $h\downarrow0$, so dominated convergence gives the right derivative. In
the same way, for $0<h<K$,
$\big(C(K)-C(K-h)\big)/h=-B\,\EQ\big[\min\big((S_T-K+h)^+,h\big)/h\big]$, and the variable
inside converges to $\1_{\{S_T\ge K\}}$.

(ii) With a density, $\Q(S_T=K)=0$ for every $K$, so by (i) $C$ is differentiable on
$(0,\infty)$ with $C'(K)=-B\int_K^\infty q_T(y)\,\dd y$. By the fundamental theorem of
calculus, $C'$ is differentiable at every point of $I$, where $q_T$ is continuous, with
derivative $B\,q_T(K)$, a continuous function on $I$.

(iii) By (i), $C'(K)=\partial^+C(K)=-B\,\Q(S_T>K)$ for $K\in I$. For $a<b$ in $I$,
$\Q(a<S_T\le b)=\big(C'(b)-C'(a)\big)/B=B^{-1}\int_a^bC''(K)\,\dd K$, by absolute continuity.
\end{proof}

So the curvature of the call price is the risk-neutral density, up to the factor $B$, and
convexity in the strike (\cref{prop:arb-strike}) is the statement that this density is
nonnegative. A butterfly with strikes $K-h$, $K$, $K+h$ costs approximately $B\,h^2q_T(K)$;
divided by $h$, it is the price of a bet that $S_T$ ends near $K$, the continuous counterpart
of the weights in the proof of \cref{prop:arb-sufficient}. The law of $S_T$ under $\Q$ need not
be its law under $\Prob$: the risk-neutral density says what the market charges for payoffs,
not how likely they are. Part (iii) is the version used on market data. It needs a
differentiable call curve, which raw quotes at finitely many strikes do not provide: second
differences of quotes are only rough estimates of $C''$, and they amplify rounding errors
(\exref{ex:arb-density}). In practice one first fits an arbitrage-free curve to the quotes.

\begin{remark}[What a model adds]\label{rem:arb-model}
Everything in this chapter is static: portfolios are set up at time $0$ and never touched.
Static arguments give relations between prices, not prices (\cref{prop:arb-sufficient}).
A static portfolio of the bond and the stock pays an affine function of $S_T$, and the payoff
of a call is not affine, so in general a call cannot be replicated in this way; the exception,
when $S_T$ takes only two values, is the one-period binomial model. There are two ways
forward. One is a model for how $S$ evolves, with
trading at intermediate dates: a call can then be replicated \emph{dynamically} by the bond
and the stock, and its price is the cost of that strategy. This is the binomial model of
\cref{ch:04-binomial} and the Black--Scholes model of \cref{ch:06-black-scholes-formula}. The
other is to take option prices from the market and use \eqref{eq:arb-strip} to price other
payoffs; \cref{ch:15-volatility} does this for variance swaps.
\end{remark}
