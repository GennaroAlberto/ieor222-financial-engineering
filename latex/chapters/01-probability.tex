% Chapter 1: Probability as information.

A derivative pays an amount that depends on how prices move until its maturity. To price
and hedge it we must say what can happen, how likely each scenario is, and what is known
at each date. A probability space takes care of the first two. This chapter is mostly
about the third: information is a $\sigma$-algebra, the best estimate of a random quantity
given some information is a conditional expectation, and a trading strategy may only use
the information available when it is chosen. The last section introduces an idea that
runs through the whole course: prices are expectations under a measure other than the
real-world one, obtained by reweighting the scenarios.

We work mostly on finite probability spaces, where every statement reduces to a finite
computation, and we say at each step what changes on a general space.

\section{A finite model of an economy}\label{sec:pr-finite}

Toss a coin $n$ times. Each toss should land heads ($H$) with probability $p\in(0,1)$ and
tails ($T$) with probability $q=1-p$, independently of the others (\cref{lem:pr-tosses}
checks that the model below does this). An outcome is a word
$\omega=\omega_1\cdots\omega_n$ in the letters $H$ and $T$, and the set of outcomes is
$\Omega_n=\{H,T\}^n$. Write $H_k(\omega)$ for the number of heads among the first $k$
tosses, and set
\begin{equation}\label{eq:pr-coin}
  \Prob(\omega)=p^{H_n(\omega)}q^{\,n-H_n(\omega)},\qquad \Prob(A)=\sum_{\omega\in A}\Prob(\omega)
  \quad(A\subseteq\Omega_n),
\end{equation}
where $\Prob(\omega)$ is short for $\Prob(\{\omega\})$. We call $\Prob$ the
\emph{$p$-coin}. To turn the coin into a market, fix $S_0>0$ and $0<d<u$, and let a stock
price be multiplied by $u$ after each head and by $d$ after each tail:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:pr-stock}
  S_k(\omega)=S_0\,u^{H_k(\omega)}d^{\,k-H_k(\omega)},\qquad k=0,1,\dots,n .
\end{equation}
This is the binomial model of \cref{ch:04-binomial}. In our smallest example, $n=2$,
$S_0=4$, $u=\frac32$ and $d=\frac12$: then $S_1$ is $6$ or $2$ according to the first toss,
and $S_2(HH)=9$, $S_2(HT)=S_2(TH)=3$, $S_2(TT)=1$.

\begin{definition}[$\sigma$-algebra, probability space]\label{def:pr-sigma-algebra}
Let $\Omega$ be a nonempty set. A collection $\F$ of subsets of $\Omega$ is a
\emph{$\sigma$-algebra} if (i) $\Omega\in\F$; (ii) $A\in\F$ implies $A^c=\Omega\setminus
A\in\F$; (iii) $A_1,A_2,\ldots\in\F$ implies $\bigcup_{j\ge1}A_j\in\F$. The sets in $\F$
are \emph{events}, and $(\Omega,\F)$ is a \emph{measurable space}. A \emph{probability
measure} on $(\Omega,\F)$ is a map $\Prob:\F\to[0,1]$ with $\Prob(\Omega)=1$ and
$\Prob\big(\bigcup_jA_j\big)=\sum_j\Prob(A_j)$ for every sequence of pairwise disjoint events
(\emph{countable additivity}). The triple $(\Omega,\F,\Prob)$ is a \emph{probability space}.
\end{definition}

On a finite $\Omega$ we take $\F=2^\Omega$, and a probability measure amounts to weights
$\Prob(\omega)\ge0$ summing to one, as in \eqref{eq:pr-coin}. On $[0,1)$, no countably
additive probability defined on all subsets is invariant under translations mod~$1$ (Vitali;
see \textcite{williams}, Chapter~1 and Appendix~A1), which is why $\F$ may be smaller than
the power set. Countable unions are needed so that limiting events, such as
$\bigcup_k\{S_k>b\}$ with infinitely many dates, are events (\exref{ex:pr-algebras} shows
that finite unions are not enough). In finance, $(\Omega,\F,\Prob)$
models the economy: an outcome is a complete scenario up to the horizon, and $\Prob$, the
\emph{real-world} or \emph{historical} probability, gives the frequencies of the
scenarios. Other measures will appear; the letter $\Prob$ is reserved for this one.

\section{Information}\label{sec:pr-information}

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{$\sigma$}{Sigma}-algebras as information}

Suppose that an outcome $\omega$ has been drawn but we are told only the first toss. Some
events are then \emph{resolved}: we know for sure whether they occurred. In the two-toss
model, $A_H=\{HH,HT\}$ and $A_T=\{TH,TT\}$ are resolved after the first toss, while
$\{HH\}$ is not. The resolved events form a $\sigma$-algebra: if we know whether $A$
occurred, we know whether $A^c$ did, and if we know it for each of $A_1,A_2,\ldots$, we
know whether one of them did. We therefore model \emph{information} by a $\sigma$-algebra
$\mathcal G\subseteq\F$, the events that the information decides. In the two-toss model,
the information at dates $0$, $1$, $2$ is
\[
  \F_0=\{\emptyset,\Omega_2\},\qquad
  \F_1=\{\emptyset,A_H,A_T,\Omega_2\},\qquad
  \F_2=2^{\Omega_2}\ \ (16\text{ events}),
\]
and more information means more events: $\F_0\subseteq\F_1\subseteq\F_2$.

The \emph{$\sigma$-algebra generated} by a collection $\mathcal C$ of subsets,
$\sigma(\mathcal C)$, is the intersection of all $\sigma$-algebras containing $\mathcal C$,
hence the smallest one: the information obtained by learning, for each set in $\mathcal C$,
whether it occurred. The \emph{Borel $\sigma$-algebra} $\mathcal B(\R^k)$ is generated by
the rectangles of intervals, and $f:\R^k\to\R$ is \emph{Borel} if $f^{-1}(B)\in\mathcal
B(\R^k)$ for $B\in\mathcal B(\R)$. Continuous functions, indicators of Borel sets, and sums,
products and limits of Borel functions are Borel (\textcite{durrett}, Section~1.3).

\begin{definition}[Random variables, $\sigma(X)$, measurability]\label{def:pr-measurable}
Let $(\Omega,\F)$ be a measurable space and $\mathcal G\subseteq\F$ a $\sigma$-algebra.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
  \item A \emph{random variable} is a function $X:\Omega\to\R$ such that
    $\{X\in B\}:=\{\omega:X(\omega)\in B\}\in\F$ for every Borel set $B\subseteq\R$. A
    \emph{random vector} $X:\Omega\to\R^k$ satisfies this for every $B\in\mathcal B(\R^k)$.
  \item The \emph{$\sigma$-algebra generated by $X$} is
    $\sigma(X)=\big\{\{X\in B\}:B\in\mathcal B(\R)\big\}$ (with $\mathcal B(\R^k)$ for a
    random vector), and $\sigma(X_1,\dots,X_k)=\sigma\big((X_1,\dots,X_k)\big)$.
  \item $X$ is \emph{$\mathcal G$-measurable} if $\sigma(X)\subseteq\mathcal G$, that is,
    $\{X\in B\}\in\mathcal G$ for every Borel set $B$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

Preimages commute with complements and unions, so $\sigma(X)$ is a $\sigma$-algebra (with
intervals alone in place of Borel sets, it would not be closed under unions). It is the
information revealed by observing $X$, and ``$X$ is $\mathcal G$-measurable'' reads:
\emph{$X$ is known once $\mathcal G$ is known}. It suffices that $\{X\le b\}\in\mathcal G$
for all $b$, because the sets $B$ with $\{X\in B\}\in\mathcal G$ form a $\sigma$-algebra.
Likewise, with rectangles, a random vector is $\mathcal G$-measurable if and only if each
coordinate is, and $\sigma(X_1,\dots,X_k)$ is the smallest $\sigma$-algebra containing
every $\sigma(X_i)$.

\subsection{Partitions}

A \emph{partition} of $\Omega$ is a finite collection $\mathcal P=\{A_1,\dots,A_m\}$ of
nonempty, pairwise disjoint events with union $\Omega$; its elements are \emph{atoms}. The
unions of atoms, including $\emptyset$, form a $\sigma$-algebra with $2^m$ elements, which
is $\sigma(\mathcal P)$. Knowing $\sigma(\mathcal P)$ means knowing which atom contains
$\omega$, and nothing more.

\begin{lemma}[Information on a finite space]\label{lem:pr-partition}
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
  \item If a $\sigma$-algebra $\mathcal G$ has finitely many elements (for instance, if
    $\Omega$ is finite), there is exactly one partition $\mathcal P$ with
    $\mathcal G=\sigma(\mathcal P)$; its atoms are the minimal nonempty sets of $\mathcal G$.
  \item A random variable is $\sigma(\mathcal P)$-measurable if and only if it is constant on
    each atom of $\mathcal P$.
  \item If $X$ takes finitely many values, $\sigma(X)$ is generated by the partition into the
    level sets $\{X=x\}$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
(a) Let $A(\omega)$ be the intersection of the finitely many sets of $\mathcal G$ containing
$\omega$, the smallest such set. If $\omega'\in A(\omega)$, then
$A(\omega')\subseteq A(\omega)$; and $\omega\in A(\omega')$, since otherwise
$A(\omega)\setminus A(\omega')$ would be a smaller set of $\mathcal G$ containing $\omega$.
So $A(\omega')=A(\omega)$. Hence two such sets are equal or disjoint (a common point
$\omega''$ gives $A(\omega)=A(\omega'')=A(\omega')$): they form a partition, and each
$A\in\mathcal G$ is the union of the $A(\omega)$ with $\omega\in A$. In any partition
generating $\mathcal G$ the atoms are the minimal nonempty sets of $\mathcal G$, which gives
uniqueness. (b) If $X=c_i$ on $A_i$, then $\{X\in B\}=\bigcup_{i:\,c_i\in B}A_i$.
Conversely, if $X(\omega)\ne X(\omega')$ with $\omega,\omega'$ in an atom $A$, then
$\{X=X(\omega)\}$ is a union of atoms containing $\omega$, hence containing $A\ni\omega'$, a
contradiction. (c) The level sets lie in $\sigma(X)$, and by (b) $X$ is measurable with
respect to the $\sigma$-algebra they generate.
\end{proof}

On a finite space, information is a partition, and more information is a finer one:
$\mathcal G\subseteq\mathcal H$ if and only if each atom of $\mathcal G$ is a union of atoms
of $\mathcal H$.

\begin{example}[What a price reveals]\label{exa:pr-two-tosses}
In the two-toss model, the atoms of $\F_1$ are $A_H$ and $A_T$, and those of $\F_2$ are the
singletons. Since $S_2$ takes the values $9$, $3$, $1$, the atoms of $\sigma(S_2)$ are
$\{HH\}$, $\{HT,TH\}$, $\{TT\}$: observing $S_2$ reveals how many heads occurred, but not
in which order. So $S_2$ is $\F_2$- but not $\F_1$-measurable; $S_1$ is not
$\sigma(S_2)$-measurable, since it differs on $HT$ and $TH$; and neither of $\F_1$ and
$\sigma(S_2)$ contains the other.
\end{example}

\begin{proposition}[Functions of known quantities are known]\label{prop:pr-functions}
If $X_1,\dots,X_k$ are $\mathcal G$-measurable and $f:\R^k\to\R$ is Borel, then
$f(X_1,\dots,X_k)$ is $\mathcal G$-measurable.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The vector $X=(X_1,\dots,X_k)$ is $\mathcal G$-measurable, and
$\{f(X)\in B\}=\{X\in f^{-1}(B)\}$ with $f^{-1}(B)$ Borel.
\end{proof}

Conversely, by the \emph{Doob--Dynkin lemma}, a $\sigma(Y)$-measurable $X$ is $f(Y)$ for a
Borel $f$. On a finite space, $X$ is constant on each level set $\{Y=y\}$
(\cref{lem:pr-partition}): let $f(y)$ be that constant, and $0$ off the range of $Y$. The
general case is \exref{ex:pr-doob-dynkin}.

\begin{remark}[What changes on a general space]\label{rem:pr-general}
On an infinite $\Omega$, a $\sigma$-algebra need not come from a partition, and atoms may
carry no probability: if $X$ has a density, each $\{X=x\}$ is null, though observing $X$ is
very informative. Information is then described by events, and \cref{sec:pr-condexp}
defines conditional expectations through events.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Filtrations and adapted processes}

\begin{definition}[Filtration]\label{def:pr-filtration}
Let $\mathbb T\subseteq[0,\infty)$ be a set of dates, such as $\{0,\dots,n\}$, $\N$ or
$[0,T]$. A \emph{filtration} on $(\Omega,\F)$ is a family $\Fil=(\F_t)_{t\in\mathbb T}$ of
$\sigma$-algebras with $\F_s\subseteq\F_t\subseteq\F$ whenever $s\le t$.
\end{definition}

\begin{example}[The coin-toss filtration]\label{exa:pr-coin-filtration}
On $\Omega_n$, the information carried by the first $k$ tosses is the $\sigma$-algebra
$\F_k$ generated by the partition into the $2^k$ sets
$A_w=\{\omega:\omega_1\cdots\omega_k=w\}$, $w\in\{H,T\}^k$. Then $\F_0=\{\emptyset,\Omega_n\}$,
$\F_n=2^{\Omega_n}$, and $\F_k\subseteq\F_{k+1}$ because each atom splits in two,
$A_w=A_{wH}\cup A_{wT}$: the atoms of $\F_k$ are the nodes at depth $k$ of the binary tree of
the tosses. The information carried by the later tosses is the $\sigma$-algebra
$\mathcal G_k$ generated by the sets $\{\omega:\omega_{k+1}\cdots\omega_n=w'\}$,
$w'\in\{H,T\}^{n-k}$.
\end{example}

\begin{definition}[Stochastic process; adapted and predictable]\label{def:pr-adapted}
A \emph{stochastic process} is a family $X=(X_t)_{t\in\mathbb T}$ of random variables; for
fixed $\omega$, $t\mapsto X_t(\omega)$ is a \emph{path}. The process is \emph{adapted} to
$(\F_t)_{t\in\mathbb T}$ if $X_t$ is $\F_t$-measurable for every $t$. In discrete time,
$(X_k)_{k\ge1}$ is \emph{predictable} if $X_k$ is $\F_{k-1}$-measurable for every $k\ge1$.
\end{definition}

The \emph{natural filtration} $\F^X_t=\sigma(X_s:s\le t)$, the smallest $\sigma$-algebra
containing every $\sigma(X_s)$ with $s\le t$, is the smallest filtration to which $X$ is
adapted. In the coin-toss model the price is adapted but not predictable, and since
$u\ne d$, $S_k/S_{k-1}$ reveals the $k$-th toss, so $\F^S_k=\F_k$. Adaptedness is the form
of a constraint every trader faces: the number $\Delta_k$ of shares held from date $k$ to
$k+1$ must be chosen with what is known at date $k$. Without it there would be nothing to
study: knowing $\omega$, one would buy whatever is about to go up.

\section{Independence}\label{sec:pr-independence}

Measurability means that $\mathcal G$ determines $X$. Independence is the opposite
extreme: $\mathcal G$ says nothing about $X$.

\begin{definition}[Independence]\label{def:pr-independence}
Let $(\Omega,\F,\Prob)$ be a probability space.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
  \item $\sigma$-algebras $\mathcal G_1,\dots,\mathcal G_m\subseteq\F$ are
    \emph{independent} if $\Prob(A_1\cap\cdots\cap A_m)=\Prob(A_1)\cdots\Prob(A_m)$ for all
    $A_i\in\mathcal G_i$. An infinite family is independent if every finite subfamily is.
  \item Random variables or vectors $X_1,\dots,X_m$ are \emph{independent} if
    $\sigma(X_1),\dots,\sigma(X_m)$ are, that is,
    $\Prob(X_1\in B_1,\dots,X_m\in B_m)=\prod_i\Prob(X_i\in B_i)$ for all Borel $B_i$. A random
    variable $X$ is \emph{independent of} $\mathcal G$ if $\sigma(X)$ and $\mathcal G$ are
    independent. Events are independent if their indicators are.
\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

Functions of independent variables are independent, since $\sigma(f(X))\subseteq\sigma(X)$.
Independence is a property of the measure: the same variables can be independent under one
measure and not under another (\exref{ex:pr-two-tosses}). For variables with finitely many
values it suffices to check $\Prob(X_1=x_1,\dots,X_m=x_m)=\prod_i\Prob(X_i=x_i)$ and sum
over $x_i\in B_i$; in general, half-lines $B_i=(-\infty,b_i]$ suffice, by Dynkin's
$\pi$--$\lambda$ theorem (\textcite{durrett}, Section~2.1).

\begin{lemma}[Past and future tosses]\label{lem:pr-tosses}
Under the $p$-coin the tosses are independent with $\Prob(\omega_j=H)=p$, and for every
$k$ the $\sigma$-algebras $\F_k$ and $\mathcal G_k$ are independent.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
For a word $v$ of length $\ell$ with $h$ heads, let $\pi(v)=p^hq^{\ell-h}$. For words $w$,
$w'$ of lengths $k$, $n-k$, the event $A_w\cap\{\omega_{k+1}\cdots\omega_n=w'\}$ is the
outcome $ww'$, of probability $\pi(w)\pi(w')$. Summing over $w'$, or over $w$, gives
$\Prob(A_w)=\pi(w)$ and $\Prob(\omega_{k+1}\cdots\omega_n=w')=\pi(w')$, since
$(p+q)^\ell=1$. So the product rule holds for atoms, and by summation for all $A\in\F_k$,
$B\in\mathcal G_k$. Summing $\Prob(A_w)=\pi(w)$ over the words of length $j$ ending in $H$
gives $\Prob(\omega_j=H)=p$, so $\Prob(\omega_1=a_1,\dots,\omega_n=a_n)=\pi(a_1\cdots a_n)
=\prod_j\Prob(\omega_j=a_j)$: the tosses are independent.
\end{proof}

In particular $H_n-H_k$, the number of heads among the tosses after date $k$, is
$\mathcal G_k$-measurable, independent of $\F_k$, and binomial with parameters $n-k$, $p$.

\section{Conditional expectation}\label{sec:pr-condexp}

Between the two extremes, information tells us something about $X$ without determining it.
The conditional expectation is the best estimate of $X$ that the information allows.

\subsection{Conditioning on a partition}

For an event $A$ with $\Prob(A)>0$, the elementary conditional expectation
$\E[X\mid A]=\E[X\1_A]/\Prob(A)$ is the average of $X$ over $A$.

\begin{definition}[Conditional expectation given a partition]\label{def:pr-condexp-discrete}
Let $\mathcal P=\{A_1,\dots,A_m\}$ be a partition of $\Omega$ into events,
$\mathcal G=\sigma(\mathcal P)$, and $X$ integrable. The \emph{conditional expectation of $X$
given $\mathcal G$} is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:pr-condexp-discrete}
  \E[X\mid\mathcal G]=\sum_{i:\,\Prob(A_i)>0}\frac{\E[X\1_{A_i}]}{\Prob(A_i)}\,\1_{A_i}.
\end{equation}
If $Y$ takes finitely many values, $\E[X\mid Y]:=\E[X\mid\sigma(Y)]=\varphi(Y)$, where
$\varphi(y)=\E[X\mid Y=y]$ if $\Prob(Y=y)>0$ and $\varphi(y)=0$ otherwise.
\end{definition}

It is a random variable, not a number: once we learn which atom contains $\omega$, our
estimate of $X$ is its average over that atom. On null atoms any value would do.

\begin{example}[Two tosses]\label{exa:pr-two-tosses-ce}
Under the $p$-coin, $\E[S_2\mid\F_1]=(9p^2+3pq)/p=9p+3q$ on $A_H$ and $(3pq+q^2)/q=3p+q$ on
$A_T$. As $S_1$ is $6$ on $A_H$ and $2$ on $A_T$,
\[
  \E[S_2\mid\F_1]=(pu+qd)\,S_1=\big(\tfrac12+p\big)S_1,\qquad \E[S_2]=\big(\tfrac12+p\big)^2S_0 .
\]
For $p=\frac12$, the forecast of tomorrow's price is today's price. Conditioning can also
look backwards: on $\{S_2=3\}=\{HT,TH\}$, $\E[S_1\mid S_2]=(6pq+2pq)/(2pq)=4$ whatever $p$.
\end{example}

The variable \eqref{eq:pr-condexp-discrete} (i) is $\mathcal G$-measurable, being constant on
atoms, and (ii) satisfies $\E\big[\E[X\mid\mathcal G]\1_A\big]=\E[X\1_A]$ for every
$A\in\mathcal G$: as $A$ is a disjoint union of atoms, take $A=A_i$, where both sides equal
$\E[X\1_{A_i}]$. Conversely, an integrable $Y'$ with (i) and (ii) equals a constant $c_i$ on
each $A_i$ (\cref{lem:pr-partition}), and (ii) with $A=A_i$ gives
$c_i\Prob(A_i)=\E[X\1_{A_i}]$: $Y'$ agrees with \eqref{eq:pr-condexp-discrete} on every atom
of positive probability, that is, almost surely.

\subsection{The general definition}

Properties (i) and (ii) make sense for any $\sigma$-algebra, with no atoms in sight, and
they determine the conditional expectation up to a null set. They become the definition.

\begin{definition}[Conditional expectation]\label{def:pr-condexp}
Let $X$ be an integrable random variable on $(\Omega,\F,\Prob)$ and $\mathcal G\subseteq\F$
a $\sigma$-algebra. A \emph{conditional expectation of $X$ given $\mathcal G$} is an
integrable random variable $Y$ such that
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
  \item $Y$ is $\mathcal G$-measurable;
  \item $\E[Y\1_A]=\E[X\1_A]$ for every $A\in\mathcal G$ \emph{(partial averaging)}.
\end{enumerate}
We write $Y=\E[X\mid\mathcal G]$, $\E[X\mid Y]=\E[X\mid\sigma(Y)]$ for a random variable or
vector $Y$, and $\Prob(A\mid\mathcal G)=\E[\1_A\mid\mathcal G]$.
\end{definition}

The estimate uses only the information $\mathcal G$, and on every event that $\mathcal G$
decides it has the same average as $X$; with $A=\Omega$, $\E\big[\E[X\mid\mathcal G]\big]=\E[X]$.

\begin{theorem}[Existence and uniqueness]\label{thm:pr-condexp-exists}
Let $X$ be integrable and $\mathcal G\subseteq\F$ a $\sigma$-algebra. A conditional
expectation $\E[X\mid\mathcal G]$ exists; any two are equal almost surely; and
$\E\big|\E[X\mid\mathcal G]\big|\le\E|X|$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
\emph{Uniqueness.} Let $Y,Y'$ be two conditional expectations and $A=\{Y>Y'\}$, which is in
$\mathcal G$ by \cref{prop:pr-functions}. By (ii), $W=(Y-Y')\1_A\ge0$ has
$\E[W]=\E[X\1_A]-\E[X\1_A]=0$. Then $W=0$ almost surely: by Markov's inequality
$\Prob(W\ge1/j)\le j\,\E[W]=0$ for all $j\ge1$, and $\{W>0\}=\bigcup_j\{W\ge1/j\}$. As $W>0$
on $A$, $\Prob(A)=0$; exchanging $Y$ and $Y'$ gives $\Prob(Y<Y')=0$. \emph{The bound.} With
$A=\{Y>0\}\in\mathcal G$, $\E|Y|=\E[Y\1_A]-\E[Y\1_{A^c}]=\E[X\1_A]-\E[X\1_{A^c}]\le\E|X|$.
\emph{Existence.} For partitions, and so on finite spaces, \eqref{eq:pr-condexp-discrete}
works. In general, for $X\ge0$, $\nu(A)=\E[X\1_A]$ is a finite measure on $(\Omega,\mathcal
G)$ vanishing on $\Prob$-null sets, and its Radon--Nikodym density with respect to $\Prob$ on
$\mathcal G$ satisfies (i) and (ii); for general $X$, use $X=X^+-X^-$. See
\textcite{durrett}, Section~4.1, or \textcite{williams}, Sections~6.11 and~9.5, which avoids
Radon--Nikodym by starting from the existence of orthogonal projections in $L^2$; compare
\cref{prop:pr-projection}.
\end{proof}

Every identity or inequality between conditional expectations holds almost surely; we
will not repeat it. If every outcome of a finite space has positive probability, ``almost
surely'' means ``everywhere''.

\subsection{Properties}

\begin{proposition}[Properties of conditional expectation]\label{prop:pr-condexp-properties}
Let $X$ and $Y$ be integrable, $\mathcal G,\mathcal H\subseteq\F$ $\sigma$-algebras, and
$a,b\in\R$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
  \item \emph{Linearity:} $\E[aX+bY\mid\mathcal G]=a\E[X\mid\mathcal G]+b\E[Y\mid\mathcal G]$.
  \item \emph{Positivity:} if $X\ge0$, then $\E[X\mid\mathcal G]\ge0$. Hence $X\le Y$ implies
    $\E[X\mid\mathcal G]\le\E[Y\mid\mathcal G]$, and
    $\big|\E[X\mid\mathcal G]\big|\le\E\big[|X|\,\big|\,\mathcal G\big]$.
  \item \emph{Taking out what is known:} if $Z$ is $\mathcal G$-measurable and $ZX$ is
    integrable (for instance, if $Z$ is bounded), then
    $\E[ZX\mid\mathcal G]=Z\,\E[X\mid\mathcal G]$. In particular,
    $\E[Z\mid\mathcal G]=Z$ for every integrable $\mathcal G$-measurable $Z$.
  \item \emph{Tower property:} if $\mathcal H\subseteq\mathcal G$, then
    $\E\big[\E[X\mid\mathcal G]\,\big|\,\mathcal H\big]=\E[X\mid\mathcal H]$.
  \item \emph{Independence:} if $X$ is independent of $\mathcal G$, then
    $\E[X\mid\mathcal G]=\E[X]$.
  \item \emph{Conditional Jensen:} if $\phi:\R\to\R$ is convex and $\phi(X)$ is integrable,
    then $\phi\big(\E[X\mid\mathcal G]\big)\le\E[\phi(X)\mid\mathcal G]$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Each time we check (i) and (ii) of \cref{def:pr-condexp} for a candidate and conclude by
uniqueness. (i) $a\E[X\mid\mathcal G]+b\E[Y\mid\mathcal G]$ is $\mathcal G$-measurable, and its integral
over $A\in\mathcal G$ is $a\E[X\1_A]+b\E[Y\1_A]=\E[(aX+bY)\1_A]$.

(ii) Let $W=\E[X\mid\mathcal G]$ and $A=\{W<0\}\in\mathcal G$. Then $W\1_A\le0$ while
$\E[W\1_A]=\E[X\1_A]\ge0$, so $W\1_A=0$ almost surely and $\Prob(A)=0$. The other claims
follow by applying this to $Y-X$ and to $|X|\pm X$. (iii) Let $W=\E[X\mid\mathcal G]$. If $Z=\1_B$ with $B\in\mathcal G$, then for
$A\in\mathcal G$, $\E[\1_BW\1_A]=\E[W\1_{A\cap B}]=\E[X\1_{A\cap B}]=\E[\1_BX\1_A]$. By
linearity the claim holds for every $\mathcal G$-measurable $Z$ with finitely many values,
which on a finite space is every $Z$. In general, let first $X,Z\ge0$, and let
$Z_j=\min(j,2^{-j}\lfloor2^jZ\rfloor)$; these take finitely many values, are
$\mathcal G$-measurable, and increase to $Z$. For $A\in\mathcal G$,
$\E[Z_jW\1_A]=\E[Z_jX\1_A]$, and $W\ge0$ by (ii), so monotone convergence gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:pr-takeout-positive}
  \E\big[ZW\1_A\big]=\E\big[ZX\1_A\big],
\end{equation}
an identity in $[0,\infty]$ that holds even if $ZX$ is not integrable. Here it is, so
$A=\Omega$ shows $ZW$ integrable, and $ZW=\E[ZX\mid\mathcal G]$. For general signs, each of
the four products $Z^\pm X^\pm$ is integrable, being at most $|ZX|$; the nonnegative case
and (i) give $\E[ZX\mid\mathcal G]=(Z^+-Z^-)\big(\E[X^+\mid\mathcal G]-\E[X^-\mid\mathcal G]\big)
=ZW$.

(iv) $\E[X\mid\mathcal H]$ is $\mathcal H$-measurable, and for $A\in\mathcal H\subseteq\mathcal G$,
$\E\big[\E[X\mid\mathcal H]\1_A\big]=\E[X\1_A]=\E\big[\E[X\mid\mathcal G]\1_A\big]$, which is
the defining property of $\E\big[\E[X\mid\mathcal G]\,\big|\,\mathcal H\big]$.

(v) We need $\E[X\1_A]=\E[X]\Prob(A)$ for $A\in\mathcal G$. If $X$ takes finitely many
values, this is the product rule for $\{X=x\}$ and $A$, summed over $x$. For $X\ge0$, apply
it to the functions $X_j=\min(j,2^{-j}\lfloor2^jX\rfloor)$ of $X$, which increase to $X$,
and use monotone convergence. In general, use $X=X^+-X^-$.

(vi) A convex $\phi$ on $\R$ is continuous and lies above its tangent lines: with $a_c$ the
right derivative at $c$, $\phi(x)\ge\phi(c)+a_c(x-c)$ for all $x$, and $c\mapsto a_c$ is
nondecreasing, hence bounded on bounded sets. By (i) and (ii), for each $c$,
$\E[\phi(X)\mid\mathcal G]\ge\phi(c)+a_c\big(\E[X\mid\mathcal G]-c\big)$ outside a null set.
Outside the union of the countably many null sets for rational $c$, this holds for all
rational $c$ at once; letting $c\to\E[X\mid\mathcal G]$ along the rationals, the right side
tends to $\phi(\E[X\mid\mathcal G])$.
\end{proof}

On a finite space, $\E[\,\cdot\mid\mathcal G]$ is on each atom $A_i$ of positive probability
the expectation under $\Prob(\,\cdot\mid A_i)$, so (i), (ii) and (vi) are properties of
ordinary expectations, atom by atom. If $\mathcal G\subseteq\mathcal H$, (iii) gives
$\E\big[\E[X\mid\mathcal G]\,\big|\,\mathcal H\big]=\E[X\mid\mathcal G]$, so with (iv) the
smaller $\sigma$-algebra wins in either order; when neither contains the other, the order
matters (\exref{ex:pr-four-points}).

\begin{remark}[Nonnegative variables]\label{rem:pr-nonnegative}
For $X\ge0$, not necessarily integrable, $\E[X\mid\mathcal G]$ is defined with values in
$[0,\infty]$ as the limit of $\E[\min(X,j)\mid\mathcal G]$, which is nondecreasing in $j$ by
positivity. By monotone convergence on both sides, it is $\mathcal G$-measurable and satisfies
(ii) of \cref{def:pr-condexp} as an identity in $[0,\infty]$. Any other $\mathcal
G$-measurable $Y'\ge0$ with this property agrees with it almost surely: with $Y$ the limit
and $A=\{Y'<Y,\ Y'\le m\}\in\mathcal G$, $\E[Y\1_A]=\E[Y'\1_A]\le m$, so $(Y-Y')\1_A\ge0$
has zero mean and $\Prob(A)=0$; let $m\to\infty$ and exchange $Y$, $Y'$. When $X$ is
integrable this is the usual $\E[X\mid\mathcal G]$, and \eqref{eq:pr-takeout-positive} says
that $\E[ZX\mid\mathcal G]=Z\,\E[X\mid\mathcal G]$ for all $\mathcal G$-measurable $Z\ge0$.
\end{remark}

\subsection{The independence lemma}

To condition on $\mathcal G$, freeze what $\mathcal G$ knows and average over what it does
not.

\begin{lemma}[Independence lemma]\label{lem:pr-independence}
Let $\mathcal G\subseteq\F$, let $X$ be a $\mathcal G$-measurable random vector in $\R^k$ and
$Y$ a random vector in $\R^l$ independent of $\mathcal G$, and let $f:\R^{k+l}\to\R$ be a
Borel function such that $f\ge0$ or $f(X,Y)$ is integrable. Then
\[
  \E\big[f(X,Y)\,\big|\,\mathcal G\big]=g(X),\qquad\text{where } g(x)=\E\big[f(x,Y)\big] .
\]
If $f\ge0$, $g$ takes values in $[0,\infty]$ and the left side is understood as in
\cref{rem:pr-nonnegative}. If $f(X,Y)$ is integrable, $f(x,Y)$ is integrable for
$\Prob_X$-almost every $x$, where $\Prob_X$ is the law of $X$; $g$ is defined at those $x$
and set to $0$ elsewhere.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
\emph{Finitely many values} (all that is needed on a finite space). Let $X$ take the
values $x_i$ and $Y$ the values $y_j$. Then
$g(x)=\sum_jf(x,y_j)\Prob(Y=y_j)$ is Borel, so $g(X)$ is $\mathcal G$-measurable. For
$A\in\mathcal G$, the event $A\cap\{X=x_i\}\in\mathcal G$ is independent of $\{Y=y_j\}$, so
\[
  \E\big[f(X,Y)\1_A\big]=\sum_{i,j}f(x_i,y_j)\Prob\big(A\cap\{X=x_i\}\big)\Prob(Y=y_j)
  =\sum_ig(x_i)\Prob\big(A\cap\{X=x_i\}\big),
\]
which is $\E[g(X)\1_A]$. \emph{General case.} Let $\mathcal D$ be the class of Borel sets
$D\subseteq\R^{k+l}$ such that $f=\1_D$ satisfies the lemma with
$g_D(x)=\Prob\big((x,Y)\in D\big)$ Borel. For a rectangle $B\times C$,
$g(x)=\1_B(x)\Prob(Y\in C)$ and, for $A\in\mathcal G$,
$\E[\1_B(X)\1_C(Y)\1_A]=\Prob(A\cap\{X\in B\})\Prob(Y\in C)=\E[g(X)\1_A]$ by independence. So
$\mathcal D$ contains the rectangles, a class closed under intersections, and $\R^{k+l}$; it
is closed under proper differences (subtract the identities) and increasing unions
(monotone convergence, in $g$ and in the identity). By Dynkin's $\pi$--$\lambda$ theorem,
$\mathcal D=\mathcal B(\R^{k+l})$. By linearity the lemma holds for nonnegative Borel $f$
with finitely many values. For Borel $f\ge0$, take such $f_j$ increasing to $f$: the $g_j$
increase to $g$, which is Borel, and monotone convergence in
$\E[f_j(X,Y)\1_A]=\E[g_j(X)\1_A]$ gives $\E[f(X,Y)\1_A]=\E[g(X)\1_A]$ in $[0,\infty]$ for
all $A\in\mathcal G$, which is the claim. If $f(X,Y)$ is integrable, apply this to $f^\pm$:
$\E[g^\pm(X)]=\E[f^\pm(X,Y)]<\infty$, so the Borel set $N$ where $g^+$ or $g^-$ is infinite
has $\Prob(X\in N)=0$. Off $N$, $f(x,Y)$ is integrable and $g=g^+-g^-$; so $g(X)=g^+(X)-g^-(X)$
almost surely, and linearity gives the claim.
\end{proof}

The lemma is how option prices at intermediate dates are computed throughout the course.

\begin{example}[Forecasting a function of the price]\label{exa:pr-markov}
Fix $k\le n$ and a function $h$, and write $S_n=S_kR$ with
$R=u^{H_n-H_k}d^{\,(n-k)-(H_n-H_k)}$. Here $S_k$ is $\F_k$-measurable, and $R$ is
$\mathcal G_k$-measurable, hence independent of $\F_k$, with $H_n-H_k$ binomial
(\cref{lem:pr-tosses}). The independence lemma with $f(x,y)=h(xy)$ gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:pr-binomial-forecast}
  \E\big[h(S_n)\,\big|\,\F_k\big]=g(S_k),\qquad
  g(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{n-k}\binom{n-k}{j}p^jq^{\,n-k-j}\,h\big(x\,u^jd^{\,n-k-j}\big).
\end{equation}
The forecast depends on the past only through the current price (the Markov property);
with $h(x)=x$, $\E[S_n\mid\F_k]=(pu+qd)^{n-k}S_k$. With $p$ replaced by the risk-neutral
probability, \eqref{eq:pr-binomial-forecast} is the binomial pricing formula of
\cref{ch:04-binomial}.
\end{example}

\subsection{The best predictor}

Write $L^2$ for the square-integrable random variables, and $L^2(\mathcal G)$ for those that
are also $\mathcal G$-measurable.

\begin{proposition}[Conditional expectation as orthogonal projection]\label{prop:pr-projection}
Let $X\in L^2$ and $\mathcal G\subseteq\F$. Then $\E[X\mid\mathcal G]\in L^2(\mathcal G)$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:pr-orthogonal}
  \E\big[(X-\E[X\mid\mathcal G])\,Z\big]=0\qquad\text{for all }Z\in L^2(\mathcal G),
\end{equation}
and for every $Z\in L^2(\mathcal G)$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:pr-pythagoras}
  \E\big[(X-Z)^2\big]=\E\big[(X-\E[X\mid\mathcal G])^2\big]+\E\big[(\E[X\mid\mathcal G]-Z)^2\big].
\end{equation}
Hence $\E[X\mid\mathcal G]$ minimizes $\E[(X-Z)^2]$ over $L^2(\mathcal G)$, and any other
minimizer equals it almost surely.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Let $Y=\E[X\mid\mathcal G]$. Conditional Jensen with $\phi(x)=x^2$ gives
$Y^2\le\E[X^2\mid\mathcal G]$, so $\E[Y^2]\le\E[X^2]$. For $Z\in L^2(\mathcal G)$, $ZX$ is
integrable since $|ZX|\le\frac12(Z^2+X^2)$, and by the tower property and taking out what
is known, $\E[ZX]=\E\big[\E[ZX\mid\mathcal G]\big]=\E[ZY]$: this is \eqref{eq:pr-orthogonal}.
Expanding $(X-Z)^2=\big((X-Y)+(Y-Z)\big)^2$, the cross term vanishes by
\eqref{eq:pr-orthogonal} applied to $Y-Z$, which gives \eqref{eq:pr-pythagoras}. Its last
term is zero only if $Z=Y$ almost surely.
\end{proof}

So $\E[X\mid\mathcal G]$ is the orthogonal projection of $X$ onto $L^2(\mathcal G)$: the best
mean-square prediction of $X$ that uses only the information $\mathcal G$. For trivial
$\mathcal G$ the predictors are constants, and the best is $\E[X]$, with error $\Var(X)$.
Taking $Z=\E[X]$ in \eqref{eq:pr-pythagoras} splits the variance into residual and
explained parts, $\Var(X)=\E\big[(X-\E[X\mid\mathcal G])^2\big]+\Var\big(\E[X\mid\mathcal
G]\big)$ (\exref{ex:pr-conditional-variance}). For $\mathcal G=\sigma(Y)$, $L^2(\mathcal G)$
consists of the square-integrable functions of $Y$ (Doob--Dynkin): $\E[X\mid Y]$ is the best
predictor among \emph{all} functions of $Y$, the target of nonparametric regression. Linear
regression projects onto the affine functions $a+bY$ only, and by \eqref{eq:pr-pythagoras}
its mean squared error is larger by $\E\big[(\E[X\mid Y]-a-bY)^2\big]$; the two agree
exactly when $\E[X\mid Y]$ is affine, as for Gaussian vectors (\exref{ex:pr-density}).
\Cref{fig:pr-projection} shows a case where they do not.

\section{Changing the probability measure}\label{sec:pr-change}

In the binomial model, prices will be expectations under a coin whose probability of
heads differs from the real-world $p$ (\cref{ch:04-binomial}); in continuous time, under a
measure that changes the drift of the stock (\cref{ch:11-risk-neutral}). Outcomes, events
and information stay the same; only the weights of the outcomes change.

\begin{definition}[Equivalent measures; Radon--Nikodym derivative]\label{def:pr-equivalent}
Probability measures $\Prob$ and $\Q$ on $(\Omega,\F)$ are \emph{equivalent} if they have the
same null sets: $\Prob(A)=0$ if and only if $\Q(A)=0$. Let $\Omega$ be finite, $\F=2^\Omega$,
and $\Prob(\omega)>0$ for every $\omega$. Then $\Q$ is equivalent to $\Prob$ if and only if
$\Q(\omega)>0$ for every $\omega$, and the \emph{Radon--Nikodym derivative} of $\Q$ with
respect to $\Prob$ is the random variable
\[
  \frac{\dd\Q}{\dd\Prob}(\omega)=Z(\omega):=\frac{\Q(\omega)}{\Prob(\omega)} .
\]
\end{definition}

(If every $\Q(\omega)>0$, the only null set of either measure is $\emptyset$; outcomes that
$\Prob$ rules out can be removed from $\Omega$.) Equivalent measures agree on what is
possible, not on how likely it is. Each outcome is reweighted by $Z$:
$\Q(\omega)=Z(\omega)\Prob(\omega)$, so $Z>0$ and $\E[Z]=1$. For example,
the $p$-coin $\Prob$ and the $\tilde p$-coin $\Q$ on $\Omega_n$, with $p,\tilde p\in(0,1)$ and
$\tilde q=1-\tilde p$, are equivalent, and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:pr-coin-density}
  \frac{\dd\Q}{\dd\Prob}=\Big(\frac{\tilde p}{p}\Big)^{H_n}\Big(\frac{\tilde q}{q}\Big)^{n-H_n}.
\end{equation}

\begin{proposition}[Change of measure]\label{prop:pr-change-of-measure}
Let $Z\ge0$ be a random variable on $(\Omega,\F,\Prob)$ with $\E[Z]=1$, and let
$\Q(A)=\E[Z\1_A]$ for $A\in\F$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
  \item $\Q$ is a probability measure, every $\Prob$-null set is $\Q$-null, and
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:pr-change}
      \EQ[X]=\E[ZX]\qquad\text{for every }X\ge0\text{ and every }X\text{ with }\E\big[Z|X|\big]<\infty .
    \end{equation}
  \item If $Z>0$ almost surely, then $\Q$ is equivalent to $\Prob$, and $\E[X]=\EQ[X/Z]$ for
    $X\ge0$ and for integrable $X$: $\dd\Prob/\dd\Q=1/Z$.
  \item On a finite space as in \cref{def:pr-equivalent}, every $\Q$ equivalent to $\Prob$ is of
    this form, with $Z=\dd\Q/\dd\Prob$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
On a finite space, $\EQ[X]=\sum_\omega X(\omega)Z(\omega)\Prob(\omega)=\E[ZX]$, which is
(c) and \eqref{eq:pr-change}. In general, $\Q(\Omega)=\E[Z]=1$, and countable additivity
follows from $Z\1_{\bigcup_jA_j}=\sum_jZ\1_{A_j}$ (disjoint $A_j$) by monotone
convergence. If $\Prob(A)=0$, then $Z\1_A=0$ almost surely and $\Q(A)=0$. Identity
\eqref{eq:pr-change} holds for indicators, then for finitely many values by linearity, for
$X\ge0$ by monotone convergence, and in general by $X=X^+-X^-$. (b) If $\Q(A)=0$, then
$Z\1_A=0$ almost surely, so $\Prob(A)=0$; and \eqref{eq:pr-change} for $X/Z$ gives
$\EQ[X/Z]=\E[X]$.
\end{proof}

On a general space, the converse is the Radon--Nikodym theorem: if $\Q$ is equivalent to
$\Prob$, there is $Z>0$, unique almost surely, with $\E[Z]=1$ and $\Q(A)=\E[Z\1_A]$ for all
$A\in\F$, written $\dd\Q/\dd\Prob$ (\textcite{shreve2}, Section~1.6). A change of measure
leaves alone events, random variables, filtrations and measurability, and between
equivalent measures ``almost surely'' means the same thing. What changes are expectations,
laws, independence, and conditional expectations, which transform as follows.

\begin{proposition}[Bayes' formula]\label{prop:pr-bayes}
Let $\Omega$ be finite, $\F=2^\Omega$, $\Prob$ and $\Q$ equivalent with $\Prob(\omega)>0$ for
every $\omega$, and $Z=\dd\Q/\dd\Prob$. For every $\sigma$-algebra $\mathcal G$ and every
random variable $X$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:pr-bayes}
  \EQ[X\mid\mathcal G]=\frac{\E[ZX\mid\mathcal G]}{\E[Z\mid\mathcal G]} .
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
By \cref{lem:pr-partition}, $\mathcal G=\sigma(\mathcal P)$ for a partition $\mathcal P$,
whose atoms have positive probability under both measures. On an atom $A$, by
\eqref{eq:pr-condexp-discrete} under $\Q$ and \eqref{eq:pr-change},
\[
  \EQ[X\mid\mathcal G]=\frac{\EQ[X\1_A]}{\Q(A)}=\frac{\E[ZX\1_A]}{\E[Z\1_A]}
  =\frac{\E[ZX\1_A]/\Prob(A)}{\E[Z\1_A]/\Prob(A)}=\frac{\E[ZX\mid\mathcal G]}{\E[Z\mid\mathcal G]} .
  \qedhere
\]
\end{proof}

On each atom, the $\Q$-average of $X$ is the $Z$-weighted $\Prob$-average, normalized by
the average weight. On a general space, with $Z$ as in \cref{prop:pr-change-of-measure} and
$\EQ|X|<\infty$, \eqref{eq:pr-bayes} holds $\Q$-almost surely (\exref{ex:pr-bayes-general};
\textcite{shreve2}, Lemma~5.2.2, is the special case $Z>0$ in density-process form).

\begin{remark}[The density process]\label{rem:pr-density-process}
For $A\in\mathcal G$, $\Q(A)=\E\big[\E[Z\mid\mathcal G]\1_A\big]$: on the information
$\mathcal G$, the density of $\Q$ is $\E[Z\mid\mathcal G]$. Given a filtration, the
\emph{density process} $Z_k=\E[Z\mid\F_k]$ satisfies $\E[Z_j\mid\F_k]=Z_k$ for $k\le j$ (a
martingale, \cref{ch:02-martingales}). For $\F_j$-measurable $X$ and $k\le j$,
$\E[ZX\mid\F_k]=\E\big[\E[ZX\mid\F_j]\,\big|\,\F_k\big]=\E[Z_jX\mid\F_k]$, so Bayes' formula
reads $\EQ[X\mid\F_k]=\E[Z_jX\mid\F_k]/Z_k$: the form used for Girsanov's theorem. For the
two coins, see \exref{ex:pr-coin-measures}.
\end{remark}

\subsection{The Gaussian shift}

On a general space, the most important change of measure is the following.

\begin{proposition}[Gaussian shift]\label{prop:pr-gaussian-shift}
Let $X\sim\mathcal N(0,1)$ under $\Prob$, $\theta\in\R$, and
\[
  Z=\exp\Big(\theta X-\frac{\theta^2}{2}\Big),\qquad \Q(A)=\E[Z\1_A],\quad A\in\F .
\]
Then $Z>0$, $\E[Z]=1$, $\Q$ is equivalent to $\Prob$, and $X\sim\mathcal N(\theta,1)$ under
$\Q$; equivalently, $X-\theta$ is standard normal under $\Q$. More precisely,
$\EQ[h(X)]=\E[h(X+\theta)]$ for every Borel $h\ge0$ and every Borel $h$ with
$\E|h(X+\theta)|<\infty$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Let $\varphi$ be the standard normal density. Completing the square gives
$e^{\theta x-\theta^2/2}\varphi(x)=\varphi(x-\theta)$, so for Borel $h\ge0$, as $X$ has
density $\varphi$ under $\Prob$,
\[
  \E\big[Z\,h(X)\big]=\int_\R h(x)\,e^{\theta x-\theta^2/2}\varphi(x)\,\dd x
  =\int_\R h(x)\varphi(x-\theta)\,\dd x=\E\big[h(X+\theta)\big].
\]
With $h=1$, $\E[Z]=1$, so \cref{prop:pr-change-of-measure} applies, and $Z>0$ makes $\Q$
equivalent to $\Prob$. By \eqref{eq:pr-change} the left side is $\EQ[h(X)]$. With
$h=\1_{(-\infty,b]}$, $\Q(X\le b)=\Prob(X+\theta\le b)$, the $\mathcal N(\theta,1)$
distribution function at $b$. For integrable $h(X+\theta)$, use $h=h^+-h^-$.
\end{proof}

For $\theta>0$, the weight $Z$ exceeds one exactly where $X>\theta/2$ (for $\theta<0$, where
$X<\theta/2$), and it moves probability in exactly the way that shifts the Gaussian without deforming it. Averaging $Z\,h(X)$ over samples drawn
under $\Prob$ estimates $\EQ[h(X)]$: this is \emph{importance sampling}
(\exref{ex:pr-importance}). The cost: $\E[Z^2]=e^{\theta^2}$, so for large $|\theta|$ a few
samples carry most of the weight.

\begin{remark}[A first look at Girsanov's theorem]\label{rem:pr-girsanov}
Let $W$ be a Brownian motion with a filtration $(\F_t)$ (\cref{def:bm,def:bm-filtration}),
$T>0$,
$\theta\in\R$, and $\dd\Q/\dd\Prob=Z_T=\exp(\theta W_T-\frac{\theta^2}2T)$. For
$0<t\le T$, the density of $\Q$ on $\F_t$ is $\E[Z_T\mid\F_t]=\exp(\theta
W_t-\frac{\theta^2}2t)$ (\cref{thm:bm-martingales}), which is the density of
\cref{prop:pr-gaussian-shift} for $X=W_t/\sqrt t$ and shift $\theta\sqrt t$; so
$W_t\sim\mathcal N(\theta t,t)$ under $\Q$. Girsanov's theorem (\cref{ch:11-risk-neutral})
shows much more: $(W_t-\theta t)_{t\le T}$ is a $\Q$-Brownian motion. An exponential change
of measure adds a drift; this is how risk-neutral pricing replaces the expected return of a
stock by the interest rate.
\end{remark}
