% Chapter 10: The Black--Scholes equation by hedging.

\Cref{ch:04-binomial} replicated every claim of the binomial model by a portfolio rebalanced
at each node, with a hedge ratio given by a discrete derivative of the price.
\Cref{ch:06-black-scholes-formula} let the tree become fine and obtained the Black--Scholes
prices as expectations under a measure $\Q$, but nothing in that chapter replicates a claim in
continuous time. With It\^o's formula (\cref{ch:08-ito-formula}) we can now trade
continuously.

The argument is that of \textcite{black-scholes} and \textcite{merton1973}. If a claim can be
replicated and its price is a smooth function $v(t,S_t)$ of time and the stock price, then two
expressions for the same process must agree: the wealth equation of the replicating portfolio
and It\^o's formula for $v(t,S_t)$. Matching their $\dd W$-terms gives the hedge,
$\Delta_t=\partial_xv(t,S_t)$; matching their $\dd t$-terms gives a partial differential
equation for $v$ in which the expected return $\mu$ of the stock does not appear. Conversely,
every solution of that equation with moderate growth gives a hedge, and the Black--Scholes
formula is one. The chapter ends with what happens when the hedge is rebalanced at finitely
many dates, as it must be in practice: the hedging error no longer vanishes, but it is of the
order of the square root of the time between rebalancing dates, and it is driven by the
gamma of the claim.

\paragraph{Setting.} Throughout, $W$ is a Brownian motion with a filtration $(\F_t)_{t\ge0}$
(\cref{def:bm-filtration}) under the real-world measure $\Prob$, and $T>0$ is a horizon. The
market consists of a money market, worth $e^{rt}$ at time $t$ for some $r\in\R$, and a stock
whose price is the geometric Brownian motion (\cref{def:bs-gbm})
\begin{equation}\label{eq:dh-stock}
  S_t=S_0\exp\Big(\sigma W_t+\Big(\mu-\frac{\sigma^2}{2}\Big)t\Big),\qquad S_0>0,\ \mu\in\R,\ \sigma>0 .
\end{equation}
By \cref{prop:itf-gbm},
\begin{equation}\label{eq:dh-dS}
  \dd S_t=\mu S_t\,\dd t+\sigma S_t\,\dd W_t,\qquad
  \dd\big(e^{-rt}S_t\big)=(\mu-r)\,e^{-rt}S_t\,\dd t+\sigma\,e^{-rt}S_t\,\dd W_t .
\end{equation}
Two facts about the law of $S$ are used repeatedly. For $u>0$, $\log S_u$ is normal with
variance $\sigma^2u>0$, so $S_u$ has a density that is positive on $(0,\infty)$. And for every
$q\in\R$, by the Gaussian moment generating function,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:dh-moments}
  \E\big[S_u^q\big]=S_0^q\exp\Big(q\Big(\mu-\frac{\sigma^2}{2}\Big)u+\frac{q^2\sigma^2}{2}\,u\Big),
\end{equation}
which is bounded for $u\in[0,T]$. As in \cref{ch:08-ito-formula}, we write
$v\in C^{1,2}([0,T)\times(0,\infty))$ when $v$ has partial derivatives $\partial_tv$,
$\partial_xv$ and $\partial_{xx}v$ that are continuous on $[0,T)\times(0,\infty)$ (one-sided in
$t$ at $t=0$).

The results of \cref{ch:08-ito-formula} are stated for processes indexed by $t\ge0$ and functions
on $[0,\infty)\times\R$. We use them on intervals $[0,t']$, for processes defined only up to time
$T$ or before $T$, and functions defined for $t<T$. This is legitimate. Multiplying the drift and
the diffusion coefficient of an It\^o process by $\1_{[0,t']}$ gives an It\^o process on
$[0,\infty)$ that agrees with the given one on $[0,t']$ and is constant afterwards. And if
$t'<t''$ and $f\in C^{1,2}([0,t'']\times\R)$, then $f(\phi(t),x)$ is in $C^{1,2}([0,\infty)\times\R)$
and agrees with $f$ on $[0,t']\times\R$, where $\phi$ is continuously differentiable, equal to the
identity on $[0,t']$ and with values in $[0,t'']$, for instance
$\phi(t)=t'+(t''-t')\big(1-e^{-(t-t')/(t''-t')}\big)$ for $t>t'$.

\section{Self-financing strategies}\label{sec:dh-strategies}

In the binomial model, a portfolio that holds $\Delta_n$ shares from time $n$ to $n+1$ and
keeps the rest of its value in the money market evolves by \eqref{eq:bin-wealth}. Over an
interval $[t,t+h]$ of continuous time, with continuous compounding, the same bookkeeping reads
\[
  X_{t+h}=\Delta_tS_{t+h}+e^{rh}\big(X_t-\Delta_tS_t\big),\quad\text{that is,}\quad
  e^{-r(t+h)}X_{t+h}-e^{-rt}X_t=\Delta_t\big(e^{-r(t+h)}S_{t+h}-e^{-rt}S_t\big),
\]
the analogue of \eqref{eq:bin-discounted-wealth}. Along a partition of $[0,t]$, the
discounted wealth is $X_0$ plus a martingale transform (\cref{def:mg-transform}) of the
discounted stock price, and as the partition is refined such sums converge to integrals with
respect to the It\^o process $e^{-rt}S_t$ (\cref{ch:08-ito-formula}). We take the limit as
the definition.

\begin{definition}[Self-financing strategy]\label{def:dh-self-financing}
A \emph{self-financing strategy} is a pair $(X_0,\Delta)$ of an initial capital $X_0\in\R$
and a progressively measurable process $\Delta=(\Delta_t)_{0\le t\le T}$
(\cref{def:ito-h2}) such that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:dh-integrability}
  \int_0^T\Delta_t^2\,\dd t<\infty\quad\text{almost surely.}
\end{equation}
$\Delta_t$ is the number of shares held at time $t$. The \emph{value} of the strategy is the
process $X$ defined, for $0\le t\le T$, by
\begin{equation}\label{eq:dh-discounted-wealth}
  e^{-rt}X_t=X_0+\int_0^t\Delta_u\,\dd\big(e^{-ru}S_u\big)
  :=X_0+\int_0^t(\mu-r)\Delta_u\,e^{-ru}S_u\,\dd u+\int_0^t\sigma\Delta_u\,e^{-ru}S_u\,\dd W_u ;
\end{equation}
the amount $X_t-\Delta_tS_t$ is held in the money market.
\end{definition}

The integrals exist. Along each path, $S$ is continuous and positive on $[0,T]$, so
$0<m_\omega\le S_u\le M_\omega$ there. Hence
$\int_0^T(\sigma\Delta_ue^{-ru}S_u)^2\,\dd u\le\sigma^2e^{2|r|T}M_\omega^2\int_0^T\Delta_u^2\,\dd u<\infty$,
so the integrand of $\dd W$ is in $\mathbb H^2_{\mathrm{loc}}$, and
$\int_0^T|\Delta_u|e^{-ru}S_u\,\dd u<\infty$ by the Cauchy--Schwarz inequality: $e^{-rt}X_t$ is an
It\^o process (\cref{def:itf-process}). For the same reason, \eqref{eq:dh-integrability} is
equivalent to $\int_0^T\Delta_t^2S_t^2\,\dd t<\infty$: it says that the dollar amount held in
the stock is square-integrable in time, which is what the stochastic integral needs.

\begin{lemma}[Wealth equation]\label{lem:dh-wealth}
Let $(X_0,\Delta)$ be a self-financing strategy. Its value $X$ is the only It\^o process such
that, almost surely, for all $t\in[0,T]$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:dh-wealth}
  X_t=X_0+\int_0^t\Delta_u\,\dd S_u+\int_0^tr\big(X_u-\Delta_uS_u\big)\,\dd u,
\end{equation}
where $\int_0^t\Delta_u\,\dd S_u=\int_0^t\mu\Delta_uS_u\,\dd u+\int_0^t\sigma\Delta_uS_u\,\dd W_u$.
Equivalently,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:dh-three-terms}
  \dd X_t=rX_t\,\dd t+(\mu-r)\Delta_tS_t\,\dd t+\sigma\Delta_tS_t\,\dd W_t .
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
$Y_t=e^{-rt}X_t$ is an It\^o process with drift $(\mu-r)\Delta_te^{-rt}S_t$ and diffusion
coefficient $\sigma\Delta_te^{-rt}S_t$ (frozen after $T$, as explained in the setting), and $e^{rt}$
is an It\^o process with no $\dd W$-part, so
$[e^{r\cdot},Y]=0$ (\cref{prop:itf-decomposition}). The product rule (\cref{cor:itf-product})
gives
\[
  \dd X_t=\dd\big(e^{rt}Y_t\big)=re^{rt}Y_t\,\dd t+e^{rt}\,\dd Y_t
  =rX_t\,\dd t+(\mu-r)\Delta_tS_t\,\dd t+\sigma\Delta_tS_t\,\dd W_t ,
\]
which is \eqref{eq:dh-three-terms}, and \eqref{eq:dh-wealth} follows by writing
$rX+(\mu-r)\Delta S=\mu\Delta S+r(X-\Delta S)$. If $X'$ is another It\^o process satisfying
\eqref{eq:dh-wealth}, then $D=X-X'$ satisfies $D_t=\int_0^trD_u\,\dd u$ for all $t$, almost
surely. Along such a path $D$ is continuous, hence continuously differentiable with $D'=rD$
and $D_0=0$; so $(e^{-rt}D_t)'=0$ and $D\equiv0$.
\end{proof}

The three terms of \eqref{eq:dh-three-terms} are the return $rX_t\,\dd t$ that the whole
wealth would earn in the money market, the risk premium $(\mu-r)\Delta_tS_t\,\dd t$ earned on
the $\Delta_tS_t$ dollars held in the stock, and the risk $\sigma\Delta_tS_t\,\dd W_t$, also
proportional to the stock position. Holding no shares gives $X_t=X_0e^{rt}$; holding one share
with $X_0=S_0$ gives $e^{-rt}X_t=S_0+(e^{-rt}S_t-S_0)$ by \eqref{eq:dh-dS}, that is, $X=S$.

\begin{remark}[Which strategies are allowed]\label{rem:dh-admissible}
Adaptedness, which progressive measurability includes, says that the position at time $t$
uses only the information in $\F_t$. Condition \eqref{eq:dh-integrability} is what the integral
needs, but it allows strategies that nobody could run. With $T=1$, \cref{exa:ito-doubling}
constructs an integrand $H\in\mathbb H^2_{\mathrm{loc}}$ with
$\int_0^1H_u\,\dd W_u=1$ almost surely. If $\mu=r$, the strategy with $X_0=0$ and
$\Delta_t=H_t/(\sigma e^{-rt}S_t)$ satisfies \eqref{eq:dh-integrability}, because $S$ is bounded
away from $0$ along each path, and \eqref{eq:dh-discounted-wealth} gives $X_1=e^r$ almost
surely: something for nothing. Like the doubling strategy of \cref{exa:mg-doubling}, it needs
unlimited credit, since its value is not bounded below. Statements that rule out arbitrage
must therefore restrict the class of strategies (\cref{ch:11-risk-neutral}). The results of
this chapter need no such restriction: they construct a strategy that replicates a claim,
rather than rule strategies out.
\end{remark}

\section{The Black--Scholes equation}\label{sec:dh-pde}

We need It\^o's formula for $v(t,S_t)$ when $v$ is defined only for $x>0$. Composing with the
exponential reduces it to It\^o's formula for Brownian motion.

\begin{lemma}[It\^o's formula for functions of the stock]\label{lem:dh-ito}
Let $v\in C^{1,2}([0,T)\times(0,\infty))$ and write
$\mathcal L^\mu v=\partial_tv+\mu x\,\partial_xv+\frac12\sigma^2x^2\,\partial_{xx}v$. Almost surely,
for every $t\in[0,T)$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:dh-ito}
  e^{-rt}v(t,S_t)=v(0,S_0)+\int_0^te^{-ru}\big(\mathcal L^\mu v-rv\big)(u,S_u)\,\dd u
  +\int_0^t\sigma e^{-ru}S_u\,\partial_xv(u,S_u)\,\dd W_u .
\end{equation}
The integrand of $\dd W$ is continuous and adapted on $[0,t]$, hence in $\mathbb H^2_{\mathrm{loc}}$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Fix $t'<T$, let $s(u,w)=S_0\exp\big(\sigma w+(\mu-\frac{\sigma^2}2)u\big)$, so that
$S_u=s(u,W_u)$, and let $f(u,w)=e^{-ru}v\big(u,s(u,w)\big)$, which is in
$C^{1,2}([0,t']\times\R)$ by the chain rule. Since $\partial_us=(\mu-\frac{\sigma^2}{2})s$,
$\partial_ws=\sigma s$ and $\partial_{ww}s=\sigma^2s$, with the derivatives of $v$ evaluated at
$(u,s(u,w))$,
\[
  \partial_uf=e^{-ru}\Big(\partial_tv-rv+\Big(\mu-\frac{\sigma^2}{2}\Big)s\,\partial_xv\Big),\quad
  \partial_wf=e^{-ru}\sigma s\,\partial_xv,\quad
  \partial_{ww}f=e^{-ru}\big(\sigma^2s^2\,\partial_{xx}v+\sigma^2s\,\partial_xv\big).
\]
So $\partial_uf+\frac12\partial_{ww}f=e^{-ru}(\mathcal L^\mu v-rv)$, and \cref{thm:itf-bm} on
$[0,t']$ gives \eqref{eq:dh-ito} for $t\le t'$. Taking $t'=T-1/m$, $m=1,2,\dots$, covers
$[0,T)$.
\end{proof}

This is the formula
$\dd v=\partial_tv\,\dd t+\partial_xv\,\dd S+\frac12\partial_{xx}v\,\dd[S,S]$ of
\cref{thm:itf-process}, with $\dd[S,S]_t=\sigma^2S_t^2\,\dd t$ (\cref{prop:itf-decomposition}),
followed by the product rule for the discount factor.

Consider a European claim that pays $g(S_T)$ at time $T$, for a Borel function
$g:(0,\infty)\to\R$, and suppose that it is \emph{replicated}: some self-financing strategy has
$X_T=g(S_T)$ almost surely. A seller who receives $X_0$ and follows the strategy can pay
$g(S_T)$ whatever happens, so $X_0$ is the cost of the claim, and $X_t$ its value at time
$t$. Following Black, Scholes and Merton, suppose further that this value depends on the past
only through the current stock price, $X_t=v(t,S_t)$, as in the recombining trees of
\cref{sec:bin-multi-period}.

\begin{theorem}[The Black--Scholes equation and the delta hedge]\label{thm:dh-bs-pde}
Let $g:(0,\infty)\to\R$ be Borel, let $(X_0,\Delta)$ be a self-financing strategy with
$X_T=g(S_T)$ almost surely, and let $v\in C^{1,2}([0,T)\times(0,\infty))$ be such that
$X_t=v(t,S_t)$ almost surely, for every $t\in[0,T)$. Then:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
  \item $v$ solves the \emph{Black--Scholes equation}
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:dh-pde}
      \partial_tv(t,x)+rx\,\partial_xv(t,x)+\frac{\sigma^2x^2}{2}\,\partial_{xx}v(t,x)=r\,v(t,x),
      \qquad(t,x)\in[0,T)\times(0,\infty);
    \end{equation}
  \item the strategy is the \emph{delta hedge}: $\Delta_t=\partial_xv(t,S_t)$ for
    $\dd t\otimes\dd\Prob$-almost every $(t,\omega)\in[0,T)\times\Omega$;
  \item if moreover $v$ is continuous on $[0,T]\times(0,\infty)$, then $v(T,x)=g(x)$ for
    Lebesgue-almost every $x>0$, and for every $x>0$ if $g$ is continuous.
\end{enumerate}
Neither the equation nor the hedge involves $\mu$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Fix $t'<T$. The processes $X$ and $v(t,S_t)$ have continuous paths on $[0,t']$ and agree
almost surely at each rational time, hence at all times in $[0,t']$, almost surely. So
$e^{-rt}X_t$, given by \eqref{eq:dh-discounted-wealth}, and $e^{-rt}v(t,S_t)$, given by
\eqref{eq:dh-ito}, are two decompositions of one It\^o process on $[0,t']$; after freezing
both at $t'$ as explained in the setting, \cref{prop:itf-decomposition}(ii) applies with $T=t'$. For $\dd t\otimes\dd\Prob$-almost every
$(u,\omega)\in[0,t']\times\Omega$ their diffusion coefficients agree,
\[
  \sigma\Delta_u\,e^{-ru}S_u=\sigma e^{-ru}S_u\,\partial_xv(u,S_u),
\]
and so do their drifts,
\[
  (\mu-r)\Delta_u\,e^{-ru}S_u=e^{-ru}\Big(\partial_tv+\mu S_u\,\partial_xv+\frac{\sigma^2S_u^2}{2}\,\partial_{xx}v-rv\Big)(u,S_u).
\]
As $\sigma e^{-ru}S_u>0$, the first identity gives $\Delta_u=\partial_xv(u,S_u)$. Substituting
it into the second, the terms $\mu S_u\partial_xv(u,S_u)$ on the two sides cancel, and what
remains is $F(u,S_u)=0$, where
$F=\partial_tv+rx\,\partial_xv+\frac12\sigma^2x^2\,\partial_{xx}v-rv$ is continuous on
$[0,T)\times(0,\infty)$. Letting $t'\uparrow T$ along a sequence proves (ii), and shows that
$F(u,S_u)=0$ for $\dd t\otimes\dd\Prob$-almost every $(u,\omega)\in[0,T)\times\Omega$.

To pass from the path to the whole half-plane, note that by Tonelli's theorem
$\int_0^T\Prob\big(F(u,S_u)\ne0\big)\,\dd u=\E\int_0^T\1_{\{F(u,S_u)\ne0\}}\,\dd u=0$, so
$\Prob(F(u,S_u)\ne0)=0$ for almost every $u\in(0,T)$. Fix such a $u$. The set
$O_u=\{x>0:F(u,x)\ne0\}$ is open, because $F(u,\cdot)$ is continuous, and $S_u$ falls in it with
probability zero. Since $S_u$ has a density that is positive on $(0,\infty)$, $O_u$ has
Lebesgue measure zero, and an open set of measure zero is empty. Hence $F(u,\cdot)=0$ for
almost every $u\in[0,T)$, and by continuity of $F$ for every $u$: this is (i).

(iii) $X$ has continuous paths on $[0,T]$, and so has $v(t,S_t)$ under the extra hypothesis, so
$X_T=\lim_{t\uparrow T}v(t,S_t)=v(T,S_T)$ almost surely. Hence $v(T,S_T)=g(S_T)$ almost surely,
so the Borel set $\{x>0:v(T,x)\ne g(x)\}$ has probability zero under the law of $S_T$, and
therefore Lebesgue measure zero. If $g$ is continuous the set is open, hence empty.
\end{proof}

The proof is short because the work is done by \cref{prop:itf-decomposition}: the hedge is
read off the $\dd W$-terms, and the equation off the $\dd t$-terms. The original argument of
\textcite{black-scholes} reads the same computation differently. The position long one claim
and short $\partial_xv(t,S_t)$ shares has no $\dd W$-term, so over a short interval it is
riskless, and a riskless position must earn the riskless rate; written out, this is
\eqref{eq:dh-pde}. The equation is linear, so a portfolio of claims is hedged by the sum of
the hedges.

\begin{remark}[The drift does not matter]\label{rem:dh-mu}
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*),leftmargin=*]
  \item \emph{Why $\mu$ drops out.} In the proof, $\mu$ enters twice: through the risk premium
    $(\mu-r)\Delta_tS_t$ earned by the shares in the portfolio \eqref{eq:dh-three-terms}, and
    through the drift $\mu S_t\,\partial_xv$ of the value of the claim \eqref{eq:dh-ito}. With
    $\Delta_t=\partial_xv(t,S_t)$ the two are equal: a portfolio that tracks the claim carries
    exactly the claim's exposure to the stock, and with it exactly the claim's share of the
    risk premium. Traders who agree on $r$ and $\sigma$ but not on $\mu$ agree on prices and
    hedges; this is \cref{rem:bin-p} in continuous time.
  \item \emph{What $\mu$ does affect.} By \eqref{eq:dh-ito} and \eqref{eq:dh-pde}, the drift of
    $v(t,S_t)$ is $rv+(\mu-r)S_t\,\partial_xv$: over a short interval the claim's expected
    return is $r$ plus the stock's excess return $\mu-r$ times the \emph{elasticity}
    $S_t\,\partial_xv/v$. For a call, $c<xN(d_+)=x\,\partial_xc$ (\cref{thm:bs-formula,prop:bs-greeks}),
    so the elasticity exceeds one: a call is a leveraged position in the stock, with a larger
    risk premium, not none. The drift also determines the probability that the claim ends
    in the money, and, in \cref{sec:dh-discrete}, the size of the error of a hedge
    rebalanced at discrete dates.
  \item \emph{Why this is fortunate.} Since $\log S_u=\log S_0+(\mu-\frac{\sigma^2}{2})u+\sigma W_u$, along
    dyadic partitions of $[0,t]$ the sum of squared log-returns converges almost surely to
    $\sigma^2t$: the drift contributes nothing, by the rules $\dd W\,\dd t=0$ and
    $\dd t\,\dd t=0$ of \cref{sec:bm-qv}, and $\sum(\sigma\,\delta W)^2\to\sigma^2t$ by
    \cref{thm:bm-qv}. One path determines $\sigma$. The natural estimate of $\mu$,
    $\log(S_t/S_0)/t+\frac{\sigma^2}{2}$, has standard deviation $\sigma/\sqrt t$: about
    $6\%$ per year with $\sigma=20\%$ and ten years of data. Prices depend on the parameter
    that is easy to estimate, not on the one that is hard.
  \item \emph{The measure $\Q$.} In \cref{ch:06-black-scholes-formula}, prices were expectations
    under a measure under which the stock has drift $r$. \Cref{thm:dh-verification}(iii)
    below shows that the two routes give the same prices, and \cref{ch:11-risk-neutral}
    constructs that measure from $\Prob$ (\cref{thm:rn-girsanov}).
\end{enumerate}
\end{remark}

\section{Verification and the Black--Scholes formula}\label{sec:dh-verification}

\Cref{thm:dh-bs-pde} says what the price and the hedge must be if a smooth price exists. The
converse is a \emph{verification} theorem: a solution of the equation, with moderate growth,
is a price, and its derivative is the hedge.

\begin{theorem}[Verification]\label{thm:dh-verification}
Let $g:(0,\infty)\to\R$ be continuous, and let $v\in C^{1,2}([0,T)\times(0,\infty))$ be
continuous on $[0,T]\times(0,\infty)$, solve \eqref{eq:dh-pde}, and satisfy $v(T,x)=g(x)$ for
$x>0$. Assume the growth condition: for some constants $C,\kappa\ge0$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:dh-growth}
  |\partial_xv(t,x)|\le C\big(1+x^\kappa+x^{-\kappa}\big),\qquad(t,x)\in[0,T)\times(0,\infty).
\end{equation}
Let $X_0=v(0,S_0)$, $\Delta_t=\partial_xv(t,S_t)$ for $t<T$, and $\Delta_T=0$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
  \item $(X_0,\Delta)$ is a self-financing strategy, and its value satisfies $X_t=v(t,S_t)$ for
    all $t\in[0,T]$, almost surely. In particular $X_T=g(S_T)$: the strategy replicates the
    claim.
  \item $\E\int_0^T\Delta_t^2S_t^2\,\dd t<\infty$.
  \item If $\mu=r$, then $(e^{-rt}X_t)_{0\le t\le T}$ is a martingale, $g(S_T)$ is integrable,
    and $v(t,S_t)=e^{-r(T-t)}\E\big[g(S_T)\,\big|\,\F_t\big]$ for every $t\in[0,T]$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
(i) $\Delta$ is continuous and adapted on $[0,T)$, so it is progressively measurable
(\cref{def:ito-h2}), and by \eqref{eq:dh-growth},
$\int_0^T\Delta_t^2\,\dd t\le C^2\int_0^T(1+S_t^\kappa+S_t^{-\kappa})^2\,\dd t<\infty$ along every path,
since $S$ is continuous and positive. By \eqref{eq:dh-pde},
$\mathcal L^\mu v-rv=(\mu-r)x\,\partial_xv$, so \cref{lem:dh-ito} reads, for $t<T$,
\[
  e^{-rt}v(t,S_t)=v(0,S_0)+\int_0^t(\mu-r)\,\partial_xv(u,S_u)\,e^{-ru}S_u\,\dd u
  +\int_0^t\sigma\,\partial_xv(u,S_u)\,e^{-ru}S_u\,\dd W_u ,
\]
which is the right side of \eqref{eq:dh-discounted-wealth}. So $X_t=v(t,S_t)$ for all $t<T$,
almost surely. As $t\uparrow T$, $X_t\to X_T$ because $X$ is continuous, and
$v(t,S_t)\to v(T,S_T)=g(S_T)$ because $v$ is continuous on $[0,T]\times(0,\infty)$.

(ii) $\Delta_t^2S_t^2\le3C^2\big(S_t^2+S_t^{2\kappa+2}+S_t^{2-2\kappa}\big)$, whose expectation is bounded
on $[0,T]$ by \eqref{eq:dh-moments}; Tonelli's theorem concludes.

(iii) If $\mu=r$, the $\dd u$-integral in \eqref{eq:dh-discounted-wealth} vanishes, and by (ii)
the integrand $\sigma\Delta_ue^{-ru}S_u$ is in $\mathbb H^2$, so $e^{-rt}X_t$ is a martingale
on $[0,T]$ (\cref{thm:ito-integral}). Its terminal value $e^{-rT}g(S_T)$ is therefore
integrable, and $e^{-rt}X_t=\E[e^{-rT}g(S_T)\mid\F_t]$; multiply by $e^{rt}$ and use (i).
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[What is proved about prices]\label{rem:dh-price}
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*),leftmargin=*]
  \item \emph{One price.} Under the hypotheses of \cref{thm:dh-verification}, suppose the
    claim can be traded at time $0$ at a price $c>v(0,S_0)$. Sell it, run the strategy of
    the theorem from $v(0,S_0)$, and put $c-v(0,S_0)$ in the money market. At time $T$ the
    strategy pays $g(S_T)$, which settles the claim, and $(c-v(0,S_0))e^{rT}>0$ is left, almost
    surely. If $c<v(0,S_0)$, reverse every position. This is the argument of
    \cref{thm:bin-one-period}, and it uses one explicit strategy, whose value is $\pm v(t,S_t)$.
    For a call, the hedge of a sale is worth $c(t,S_t)\ge0$; the reversed hedge is worth
    $-c(t,S_t)\ge-S_t$, which is not bounded below by a constant, although the call held
    alongside covers it. So $v(0,S_0)$ is the only price at which the claim can trade
    without offering a riskless profit of this kind.
  \item \emph{No arbitrage at that price.} That no other strategy makes a riskless profit when
    the claim trades at $v(0,S_0)$ is a statement about all strategies, and by
    \cref{rem:dh-admissible} it needs a restriction on them. It is the fundamental theorem
    of \cref{ch:11-risk-neutral} (\cref{thm:rn-ftap}), extended to a market that
    also trades the claim in \cref{cor:rn-extended-market}.
  \item \emph{One solution.} Could two solutions of \eqref{eq:dh-pde} with the same terminal
    condition, both satisfying \eqref{eq:dh-growth}, give two hedges with different costs? No.
    The equation does not involve $\mu$ or $S_0$, so we may apply
    \cref{thm:dh-verification}(iii) in the model with $\mu=r$ and any initial price $x>0$, and
    to the function $(s,y)\mapsto v(t+s,y)$ on the horizon $T-t$, which satisfies the same
    hypotheses. At time $0$ this gives
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:dh-feynman-kac}
      v(t,x)=e^{-r(T-t)}\,\E\Big[g\Big(x\exp\Big(\sigma W_{T-t}+\Big(r-\frac{\sigma^2}{2}\Big)(T-t)\Big)\Big)\Big],
      \qquad(t,x)\in[0,T]\times(0,\infty),
    \end{equation}
    and the right side does not depend on the solution. This is the Feynman--Kac formula for
    \eqref{eq:dh-pde}; \cref{ch:12-feynman-kac} proves it for general diffusions
    (\cref{thm:fk-feynman-kac}). Uniqueness is a statement about a class of solutions: here,
    those satisfying \eqref{eq:dh-growth}.
\end{enumerate}
\end{remark}

It remains to find solutions. \Cref{ch:06-black-scholes-formula} computed the call and put
prices as expectations; the next proposition checks that they solve the equation, with the
right terminal condition and growth, by calculus alone.

\begin{proposition}[The Black--Scholes formula solves the equation]\label{prop:dh-bs-solution}
Let $K>0$, let $c$ and $p$ be the call and put functions of \cref{thm:bs-formula}, and set
$c(T,x)=(x-K)^+$ and $p(T,x)=(K-x)^+$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
  \item $c,p\in C^{1,2}([0,T)\times(0,\infty))$, and both solve \eqref{eq:dh-pde}.
  \item $c$ and $p$ are continuous on $[0,T]\times(0,\infty)$.
  \item $0<\partial_xc<1$ and $-1<\partial_xp<0$ on $[0,T)\times(0,\infty)$.
\end{enumerate}
Consequently, the call is replicated by the strategy with $X_0=c(0,S_0)$ and
$\Delta_t=N\big(d_+(T-t,S_t)\big)$, whose value is $X_t=c(t,S_t)$, and the put by the strategy
with $X_0=p(0,S_0)$ and $\Delta_t=-N\big(-d_+(T-t,S_t)\big)$, whose value is $p(t,S_t)$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
(i) For $t<T$, $\tau=T-t>0$ and $d_\pm(\tau,x)$ are smooth functions of $(t,x)$, and so are
$c$ and $p$. By \cref{prop:bs-greeks}(i), (ii) and (iv),
\[
  \partial_tc+rx\,\partial_xc+\frac{\sigma^2x^2}{2}\,\partial_{xx}c
  =-\frac{x\sigma N'(d_+)}{2\sqrt\tau}-rKe^{-r\tau}N(d_-)+rxN(d_+)+\frac{\sigma^2x^2}{2}\,\frac{N'(d_+)}{x\sigma\sqrt\tau}
  =r\,c .
\]
The terms in $N'(d_+)$ cancel; this is where the identity $Ke^{-r\tau}N'(d_-)=xN'(d_+)$ of
\cref{prop:bs-greeks}, used to compute the theta, does its work. For the put, $p=c-w$ with
$w(t,x)=x-Ke^{-r\tau}$ by the parity of \cref{thm:bs-formula}, and $w$ solves
\eqref{eq:dh-pde}: $\partial_tw+rx\,\partial_xw=-rKe^{-r\tau}+rx=rw$. The equation is linear.

(iii) By \cref{prop:bs-greeks}(i), $\partial_xc=N(d_+)\in(0,1)$ and $\partial_xp=N(d_+)-1$.

(ii) Continuity at points with $t<T$ is part of (i). Fix $x>0$ and let $t\uparrow T$: if $x>K$,
then $d_\pm\to+\infty$ and $c(t,x)\to x-K$; if $x<K$, then $d_\pm\to-\infty$ and $c(t,x)\to0$;
if $x=K$, then $d_\pm=(r\pm\frac{\sigma^2}{2})\sqrt\tau/\sigma\to0$ and
$c(t,x)\to\frac K2-\frac K2=0$. So $c(t,x)\to(x-K)^+$. By (iii) and the mean value theorem,
$|c(t,y)-c(t,x)|\le|y-x|$ for $t<T$, and also for $t=T$. Hence
$|c(t,y)-c(T,x)|\le|y-x|+|c(t,x)-c(T,x)|\to0$ as $(t,y)\to(T,x)$. For the put, use $p=c-w$.

Finally, by (iii) the growth condition \eqref{eq:dh-growth} holds with $C=1$ and $\kappa=0$, the
payoffs are continuous, and \cref{thm:dh-verification} applies.
\end{proof}

By \cref{thm:dh-verification}(iii) in the model with $\mu=r$, $c(t,S_t)$ is also
$e^{-r(T-t)}\E[(S_T-K)^+\mid\F_t]$, the expectation computed in \cref{thm:bs-formula}: the
hedging route of this chapter and the expectation route of \cref{ch:06-black-scholes-formula}
lead to the same function. The delta of the call, $N(d_+)$, is the number of shares in the
replicating portfolio, and the rest of its value, $-Ke^{-r\tau}N(d_-)$, is borrowed: the
hedge of a call is a leveraged long position in the stock.

\begin{example}[Power claims and forwards]\label{exa:dh-power}
For $a\in\R$, the claim paying $S_T^a$ has the price $v(t,x)=e^{-\alpha(T-t)}x^a$ with
$\alpha=(1-a)(r+\frac{a\sigma^2}{2})$, found by an expectation in
\exref{ex:bs-power}. The equation is checked directly: $\partial_tv=\alpha v$,
$x\,\partial_xv=av$ and $x^2\,\partial_{xx}v=a(a-1)v$, so the left side of \eqref{eq:dh-pde} is
$(\alpha+ra+\frac{\sigma^2}{2}a(a-1))v=rv$. Here $v$ is smooth on $[0,T]\times(0,\infty)$,
$|\partial_xv|=|a|e^{-\alpha(T-t)}x^{a-1}$ satisfies \eqref{eq:dh-growth}, and the hedge
$\Delta_t=av(t,S_t)/S_t$ keeps the fixed fraction $a$ of the claim's value in the stock. For
$a=1$ the claim is the stock and $\Delta=1$; for $a=0$ it is a zero-coupon bond,
$v=e^{-r(T-t)}$ and $\Delta=0$; for $a=2$, $\Gamma=\partial_{xx}v=2e^{(r+\sigma^2)(T-t)}$.
Similarly, the forward contract $g(x)=x-K$ has $v(t,x)=x-Ke^{-r(T-t)}$ and $\Delta=1$: the static
hedge of \cref{prop:arb-forward}.
\end{example}

\section{Hedging in discrete time}\label{sec:dh-discrete}

The delta hedge trades at every instant, which no one can do. Fix $n\ge1$, let $h=T/n$ and
$t_k=kh$, and consider a seller who receives $v(0,S_0)$, holds $\delta_k=\partial_xv(t_k,S_{t_k})$
shares from each date $t_k$, $k<n$, until $t_{k+1}$, and keeps the rest of her wealth in
the money market. As in \cref{ch:04-binomial}, her wealth satisfies
\[
  X^n_0=v(0,S_0),\qquad
  X^n_{t_{k+1}}=\delta_kS_{t_{k+1}}+e^{rh}\big(X^n_{t_k}-\delta_kS_{t_k}\big),\quad0\le k\le n-1,
\]
and her \emph{discounted hedging error} is $e_n=e^{-rT}\big(X^n_T-g(S_T)\big)$. Write
\[
  \Gamma_k=\partial_{xx}v(t_k,S_{t_k}),\qquad
  R_k=\frac{e^{-rt_{k+1}}S_{t_{k+1}}}{e^{-rt_k}S_{t_k}}-1
\]
for the gamma at the rebalancing date and the discounted return over the following period. We
assume:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(H),leftmargin=*]
  \item $v$ is as in \cref{thm:dh-verification}, its partial derivatives
    $\partial_t^i\partial_x^jv$ with $2i+j\le4$ exist and are continuous on
    $[0,T]\times(0,\infty)$ (one-sided in $t$ at $0$ and $T$), and each of them is bounded by
    $C(1+x^\kappa+x^{-\kappa})$ for some constants $C,\kappa\ge0$.
\end{enumerate}
The power claims of \cref{exa:dh-power} satisfy (H). The call does not: its gamma
$N'(d_+)/(x\sigma\sqrt{T-t})$ (\cref{prop:bs-greeks}) is unbounded near $(T,K)$.

\begin{proposition}[Discrete delta hedging]\label{prop:dh-hedging-error}
Assume (H). There is a constant $C'$, depending on $v$, $S_0$, $\mu$, $r$, $\sigma$ and $T$ but
not on $n$, such that:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
  \item \emph{(gamma--theta decomposition)}
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:dh-gamma-theta}
      e_n=-\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac12\,e^{-rt_k}\,\Gamma_kS_{t_k}^2\big(R_k^2-\sigma^2h\big)+\rho_n,
      \qquad\E[\rho_n^2]\le C'h^2;
    \end{equation}
  \item $|\E[e_n]|\le C'h$, and $\E[e_n]=0$ if $\mu=r$;
  \item as $n\to\infty$, with $\Gamma=\partial_{xx}v$,
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:dh-error-variance}
      \E\big[e_n^2\big]=\frac{\sigma^4h}{2}\int_0^T\E\Big[e^{-2ru}\,\Gamma(u,S_u)^2S_u^4\Big]\,\dd u+o(h).
    \end{equation}
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
\emph{Discounted coordinates.} Let $Y_t=e^{-rt}S_t$ and
$\bar v(t,y)=e^{-rt}v(t,e^{rt}y)$, so that $\bar v(t,Y_t)=e^{-rt}v(t,S_t)$. By the chain rule,
$\partial_y\bar v(t,y)=\partial_xv(t,e^{rt}y)$ and $\partial_{yy}\bar v(t,y)=e^{rt}\partial_{xx}v(t,e^{rt}y)$,
and \eqref{eq:dh-pde}, which extends to $t=T$ by continuity, becomes
\begin{equation}\label{eq:dh-pde-discounted}
  \partial_t\bar v+\frac{\sigma^2y^2}{2}\,\partial_{yy}\bar v=0\qquad\text{on }[0,T]\times(0,\infty).
\end{equation}
Time decay of the discounted price is set by its convexity: \emph{theta pays for gamma}. By (H)
and the chain rule, the derivatives $\partial_t^i\partial_y^j\bar v$ with $2i+j\le4$ are
continuous and bounded by $C_1(1+y^q+y^{-q})$ for some constants $C_1,q$.

\emph{The error as a sum.} The discounted wealth changes by $\delta_k(Y_{t_{k+1}}-Y_{t_k})$ over
$[t_k,t_{k+1}]$, and $\bar v(0,Y_0)=X^n_0$, $\bar v(T,Y_T)=e^{-rT}g(S_T)$. Telescoping,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:dh-error-sum}
  e_n=-\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}P_k,\qquad
  P_k=\bar v(t_{k+1},Y_{t_{k+1}})-\bar v(t_k,Y_{t_k})-\delta_k\big(Y_{t_{k+1}}-Y_{t_k}\big),
\end{equation}
where $P_k$ is the discounted profit over $[t_k,t_{k+1}]$ of a long position in the claim, marked
at $v$, hedged with $\delta_k$ shares.

\emph{Taylor expansion.} Fix $k$, write $y=Y_{t_k}$ and $\eta=Y_{t_{k+1}}-y=yR_k$, and evaluate the
derivatives of $\bar v$ at $(t_k,y)$ unless indicated. Expanding first in time at $y+\eta$, then
$\partial_t\bar v(t_k,\cdot)$ and $\bar v(t_k,\cdot)$ in space around $y$, and using
$\partial_y\bar v=\delta_k$ and \eqref{eq:dh-pde-discounted},
\[
  P_k=\frac12\,\partial_{yy}\bar v\,\big(\eta^2-\sigma^2y^2h\big)+A_k+B_k,\qquad
  A_k=h\,\partial_{ty}\bar v\,\eta+\frac16\,\partial_{yyy}\bar v\,\eta^3,
\]
where the Taylor remainders satisfy
$|B_k|\le\frac12h^2\sup|\partial_{tt}\bar v|+\frac12h\eta^2\sup|\partial_{tyy}\bar v|+\frac1{24}\eta^4\sup|\partial_{y}^4\bar v|$,
the suprema being over $[t_k,t_{k+1}]\times\{y+\eta\}$ for the first and over $\{t_k\}\times[y,y+\eta]$ for
the others. Since $\partial_{yy}\bar v\,y^2=e^{-rt_k}\Gamma_kS_{t_k}^2$ and $\eta^2=y^2R_k^2$, the first
term is the summand of \eqref{eq:dh-gamma-theta}, and $\rho_n=-\sum_k(A_k+B_k)$.

\emph{Moments.} $1+R_k=\exp\big(\sigma(W_{t_{k+1}}-W_{t_k})+(\mu-r-\frac{\sigma^2}{2})h\big)$ is
independent of $\F_{t_k}$, with a law that does not depend on $k$. Expanding
$\E[(1+R_k)^m]=\exp\big(m(\mu-r-\frac{\sigma^2}{2})h+\frac{m^2\sigma^2}{2}h\big)$ in powers of $h$
gives, for $h\le T$ and constants $c_m$ not depending on $h$,
\begin{gather*}
  \E|R_k|^m\le c_mh^{m/2},\qquad |\E R_k|\le c_1|\mu-r|h,\qquad|\E R_k^3|\le c_3h^2,\\
  \big|\E R_k^2-\sigma^2h\big|\le c_2h^2,\qquad\Var(R_k^2)=2\sigma^4h^2+O(h^3);
\end{gather*}
for the first, use $|e^z-1|\le|z|e^{|z|}$ and the Cauchy--Schwarz inequality. The points between
$y$ and $y+\eta=y(1+R_k)$ lie in $[y\min(1,1+R_k),\,y\max(1,1+R_k)]$, so by the growth bounds and
\eqref{eq:dh-moments} all the coefficients and suprema above have moments of every order,
bounded uniformly in $k$ and $n$. With the Cauchy--Schwarz inequality this gives
$\E[B_k^2]\le C_2h^4$ and $\E[A_k^2]\le C_2h^3$. Moreover the coefficients of $A_k$ are
$\F_{t_k}$-measurable and $R_k$ is independent of $\F_{t_k}$, so
$\E[A_k\mid\F_{t_k}]=h\,\partial_{ty}\bar v\,y\,\E R_k+\frac16\partial_{yyy}\bar v\,y^3\,\E R_k^3$
(\cref{lem:pr-independence}), and $\E\big[\E[A_k\mid\F_{t_k}]^2\big]\le C_2h^4$.

\emph{Proof of (i).} Let $a_k=A_k-\E[A_k\mid\F_{t_k}]$. For $j<k$, $a_j$ is
$\F_{t_k}$-measurable, so $\E[a_ja_k]=\E\big[a_j\,\E[a_k\mid\F_{t_k}]\big]=0$, and
$\E\big[(\sum_ka_k)^2\big]=\sum_k\E[a_k^2]\le\sum_k\E[A_k^2]\le nC_2h^3=C_2Th^2$. By the
Cauchy--Schwarz inequality for sums,
$\E\big[(\sum_k\E[A_k\mid\F_{t_k}])^2\big]\le n\sum_k C_2h^4=C_2T^2h^2$ and
$\E\big[(\sum_kB_k)^2\big]\le C_2T^2h^2$. So $\E[\rho_n^2]\le C'h^2$.

\emph{Proof of (ii) and (iii).} Write $G_k=e^{-rt_k}\Gamma_kS_{t_k}^2$, which is
$\F_{t_k}$-measurable with $\E[G_k^2]$ bounded, and
$R_k^2-\sigma^2h=\xi_k+m_h$ with $\xi_k=R_k^2-\E R_k^2$ and $m_h=\E R_k^2-\sigma^2h=O(h^2)$. The
main term of \eqref{eq:dh-gamma-theta} is $N_n-\frac12m_h\sum_kG_k$ with
$N_n=-\frac12\sum_kG_k\xi_k$. Since $\xi_k$ is independent of $\F_{t_k}$, $\E[N_n]=0$, and the
second part has $L^2$-norm at most $\frac12|m_h|\,n\max_k\E[G_k^2]^{1/2}=O(h)$. With (i),
$\|e_n-N_n\|_2=O(h)$, which gives $|\E e_n|\le C'h$. If $\mu=r$, then $\E R_k=0$, so
$\E[\delta_k(Y_{t_{k+1}}-Y_{t_k})]=\E[\delta_kY_{t_k}]\,\E R_k=0$ and $\E[e^{-rT}X^n_T]=X^n_0$, while
$\E[e^{-rT}g(S_T)]=v(0,S_0)=X^n_0$ by \cref{thm:dh-verification}(iii); so $\E[e_n]=0$.

For (iii), the cross terms of $\E[N_n^2]$ vanish: for $j<k$, $G_j\xi_jG_k$ is
$\F_{t_k}$-measurable and $\xi_k$ is independent of $\F_{t_k}$ with mean $0$. Hence
\[
  \E[N_n^2]=\frac14\sum_k\E[G_k^2]\,\E[\xi_k^2]=\frac{\sigma^4h}{2}\big(1+O(h)\big)\,h\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\varphi(t_k),
  \qquad\varphi(u)=\E\big[e^{-2ru}\Gamma(u,S_u)^2S_u^4\big].
\]
Writing $S_u=S_0\exp(\sigma\sqrt u\,Z+(\mu-\frac{\sigma^2}{2})u)$ with $Z\sim\mathcal N(0,1)$, the
continuity and growth of $\Gamma$ and dominated convergence show that $\varphi$ is continuous on
$[0,T]$, so the Riemann sums $h\sum_k\varphi(t_k)$ converge to $\int_0^T\varphi(u)\,\dd u$ and
$\E[N_n^2]=\frac{\sigma^4h}{2}\int_0^T\varphi+o(h)$. Finally
$\big|\|e_n\|_2^2-\|N_n\|_2^2\big|\le\|e_n-N_n\|_2\,\big(\|e_n\|_2+\|N_n\|_2\big)=O(h)\,O(\sqrt h)=o(h)$.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Reading the proposition.} Over each period, a delta-hedged long position in the claim
gains $\frac12\Gamma S^2R^2$ from the move of the stock and loses
$\frac12\Gamma S^2\sigma^2h$ to the passage of time, by \eqref{eq:dh-pde-discounted}. For a
claim with positive gamma it profits when the stock moves by more than $\sigma\sqrt h$ in
either direction and loses otherwise (\cref{fig:dh-gamma-theta}, left); the seller who hedges
has the opposite profit. In continuous time these gains and losses cancel exactly. In discrete
time they cancel on average, by (ii), but not path by path: up to their small means, the terms
of \eqref{eq:dh-gamma-theta} are uncorrelated, each of variance about
$\frac12\sigma^4h^2\,\E[G_k^2]$, so their sum has a standard deviation of order $\sqrt h$. To
halve the error, rebalance four times as often.

\Cref{fig:dh-hedging-error} shows the hedge of a call, which (H) does not cover. The errors
are centred and shrink like $n^{-1/2}$: with $\mu=15\%$, the root-mean-square error is $1.83$
for monthly, $0.90$ for weekly and $0.41$ for daily rebalancing, against a premium of $10.45$.
The leading term of \eqref{eq:dh-error-variance} predicts $1.91$, $0.92$ and $0.42$. For the call
the integral in \eqref{eq:dh-error-variance} is still finite, because the gamma is large only
within a distance of order $\sqrt{T-u}$ of the strike. But the proposition does not apply, and
part of it fails: for the call, the remainder in (i) is only of order $h^{3/4}$ in $L^2$
($\E[\rho_n^2]$ falls by a factor of about $8$, not $16$, when $n$ is multiplied by $4$). What
survives is known. \textcite{zhang-thesis}, Theorem~2.4.1, showed that $\sqrt n\,\|e_n\|_2$
converges for Lipschitz payoffs, so $\E[e_n^2]$ is still of order $h$; and
\textcite{bertsimas-kogan-lo} showed that for calls and puts $\sqrt n\,e_n$ converges in law to a
mixture of centred normal laws, which the leading term of \eqref{eq:dh-error-variance}
describes. Convergence in law alone does not give the limit of $n\,\E[e_n^2]$; numerically, it is
within $3\%$ of the leading term at $n=1024$ (\cref{fig:dh-hedging-error}, right). For less
regular payoffs the rate changes: for a digital option $\E[e_n^2]$ is only of order $\sqrt h$
(\textcite{gobet-temam}, Theorem~1, with the correction of \textcite{gobet-correction}, which
leaves the rates unchanged; \exref{ex:dh-digital}). \textcite{geiss-gobet} survey these results;
they state them for $\mu=r$ and note that they extend to $\mu\ne r$, the case of
\cref{fig:dh-hedging-error}, where $\mu=15\%$.

The drift enters only through the law of the path. With $\mu=-10\%$, $5\%$ and $30\%$, $\sqrt n$
times the root-mean-square error tends to $6.78$, $7.02$ and $5.44$
(\cref{fig:dh-hedging-error}, right): a large drift sends the stock away from the strike, where
the gamma is small. The hedger does not earn the risk premium, and does not need it.

Two practical points complete the picture. First, rebalancing is not free: with proportional
transaction costs, the cost of trading $n$ times grows with $n$ while the hedging error
shrinks, and \textcite{leland} shows how to compensate the costs, to first order, by hedging
with an enlarged volatility. Second, the hedge uses $\sigma$, which must be estimated or
implied from market prices (\cref{ch:15-volatility}); hedging with the wrong volatility leaves
an error that does not vanish as $n\to\infty$, and \exref{ex:dh-robust} computes it. Positions
in other options can reduce the gamma of a book, and with it the error of
\eqref{eq:dh-gamma-theta} (\exref{ex:dh-book}).
